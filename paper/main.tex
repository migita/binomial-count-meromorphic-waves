\begin{abstract}
We study meromorphic travelling waves of
\(u_t+uu_x+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_j\partial_x^{j+1}u=0\), a
constant-coefficient family containing KdV--Burgers,
Kuramoto--Sivashinsky and Kawahara. After integration and
normalization, the profiles satisfy \(P(D)v+v^2/2=0\), where
\(D=\mathrm d/\mathrm dz\) and \(P\) is monic of degree \(p\ge2\) with
complex coefficients.

For evolution equations of even order (\(p\) odd), and for purely
dispersive equations (only odd-order derivatives), we give a complete
algebraic description of all nonconstant globally meromorphic
travelling waves: they are rational, rational in an exponential, or
elliptic, with one pole per period.

One-pole waves of these three kinds are exceptional: for \(p\ge3\) a
generic equation of the family has none. We count the exceptions as
pairs \((P,v)\), over the whole family: there are
\(\binom{2p-1}{p-1}\) with \(v\) rational in an exponential of given
rate, and \(2\binom{2p-2}{p-2}\) with \(v\) elliptic on a given generic
lattice. We also count the solitary pulses of the purely dispersive
subfamily and the fronts of the purely dissipative one (only even-order
derivatives in the linear part).

When \(2p-1\) is prime, these statements are proved and all pairs are
distinct. We conjecture them in every order.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.}
Meromorphic travelling waves; dispersive and dissipative equations;
elliptic functions; polynomial convolution; algebraic multiplicity.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Exact travelling waves of nonlinear evolution equations are usually
found one equation at a time, and usually only for special values of the
coefficients. The Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation with dispersion,
\(u_t+uu_x+u_{xx}+\sigma u_{xxx}+u_{xxxx}=0\), is a classical example.
It has nonconstant travelling waves that are polynomials in a hyperbolic
tangent for exactly four values of the dispersion coefficient, up to its
sign: \(\sigma=0\), \(4\), \(12/\sqrt{47}\) and \(16/\sqrt{73}\).
Kuramoto and Tsuzuki found the wave of the dispersionless equation
\citep{KuramotoTsuzuki1976}, Kudryashov the others
\citep{Kudryashov1988,Kudryashov1989,Kudryashov1990,Kudryashov1991}, and
Eremenko proved that, together with Kudryashov's elliptic solution and a
single rational one, they exhaust the nonconstant meromorphic travelling
waves \citep{Eremenko2006}. The classical tables list six cases at these
four values \citep{KudryashovZargaryan1996,ConteMusette2009}: two at
\(\sigma=0\), two at \(\sigma=\pm4\), and one at each of the other two
values. The four values already appear together in
\citet[Eq.~(39)]{Kudryashov1989}. The tables do not distinguish a wave
from its mirror image, which solves the equation with the opposite sign
of \(\sigma\). If the two are counted separately, the two cases with
\(\sigma=0\) are their own mirror images and the other four are not, so
the list has \(2+2\cdot4=10\) members. This paper shows that ten is
\(\binom52\): the classical list is the case \(p=3\) of a binomial
enumeration, proved for infinitely many \(p\) and conjectured for all.

We consider the evolution equations
\[
 u_t+uu_x+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_j\partial_x^{j+1}u=0,\qquad b_p\ne0,
\]
which contain the KdV--Burgers (\(p=2\)), Kuramoto--Sivashinsky
(\(p=3\)), Kawahara and Benney--Lin (\(p=4\)) and Nikolaevskiy (\(p=5\))
equations; Table~\ref{tab:named} lists the named members with \(p\le6\).
Kudryashov studied this family as a whole and found exact waves of its
KdV--Burgers, Kuramoto--Sivashinsky and Kawahara members
\citep{Kudryashov1989}. A travelling wave \(u=w(x-ct)\) satisfies, after
one integration, \(\sum_jb_jw^{(j)}-cw+w^2/2+K=0\). Shifting \(w\) by a
constant solution and normalizing the leading coefficient gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 P(D)v+\frac12v^2=0,\qquad
 P(\rho)=\rho^p+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}a_j\rho^j,\qquad p\ge2,
\end{equation}
with \(D=\mathrm d/\mathrm dz\) and complex coefficients. The
coefficients \(a_1,\ldots,a_{p-1}\) are those of the evolution equation,
up to a rescaling of \(x\), and the speed, the integration constant and
the background are absorbed into \(a_0\) (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).
Throughout, \(p\) is the order of the profile equation \eqref{eq:main},
which is one less than the order of the evolution equation:
Kuramoto--Sivashinsky is a fourth-order equation and has \(p=3\).
Phrases such as ``order three'' or ``in every order'' always refer to
\(p\).

In the evolution equation, derivatives of odd order are dispersive and
those of even order dissipative (in this sign convention a positive
coefficient of \(u_{xx}\) is gain, so dissipative includes gain). An
equation is \emph{purely dispersive} if it contains only odd-order
derivatives, as KdV and Kawahara do; then \(p\) is even and \(P\) is an
even polynomial. It is \emph{purely dissipative} if its linear part
contains only even-order derivatives, as for Kuramoto--Sivashinsky
without dispersion; then \(p\) is odd and \(P-P(0)\) is odd. These are
\emph{the two pure subfamilies}, one for each parity of \(p\). All other
equations, such as KdV--Burgers, are \emph{mixed}.

\paragraph{The waves.}
A travelling wave is \emph{meromorphic} if its profile is a
single-valued meromorphic function of the complex variable \(z\). The
familiar exact solutions are of this kind: a polynomial in \(\tanh\) or
\(\operatorname{sech}\) is a rational function of an exponential
\(e^{\kappa z}\), a cnoidal wave is an elliptic function, and rational
functions of \(z\) also occur. These are the three kinds of wave in this
paper. We consider those with \emph{one pole per period}: all the poles
of the profile are translates of a single pole by its periods. For a
wave of \(\tanh\) type the period is the imaginary period
\(2\pi i/\kappa\) of \(e^{\kappa z}\), and the rate \(\kappa\) measures
the width of the wave. Such a wave tends to a constant at each end
(Section~\ref{sec:systems}); it is a \emph{pulse} if it returns to the
same constant, and a \emph{front} if it connects two different ones.

For every equation of even order, and for every purely dispersive
equation, these are all the meromorphic travelling waves that exist
(Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}). For the remaining equations, the mixed
ones of odd order such as KdV--Burgers, other meromorphic waves can
occur; there we count the one-pole waves without claiming more. These
equations are the rows marked \(\dagger\) in Table~\ref{tab:named}.

\paragraph{The question.}
For \(p\ge3\) such waves are exceptional: an equation of the family has
one only if its coefficients satisfy algebraic relations, as the four
values of \(\sigma\) illustrate. This suggests the right question. We do
not ask how many waves a given equation has, but how many pairs of an
equation and a wave there are in the whole family.

To make this question finite, two continuous freedoms must be removed. A
translate of a wave is again a wave, so we place the pole at a fixed
point. Rescaling \(x\) changes the width of a wave and the coefficients
of its equation together, so we fix the width: for a wave that is
rational in an exponential we prescribe the rate \(\kappa=1\), and for
an elliptic wave we prescribe both of its periods, that is, its period
lattice. The coefficients of the equation remain free. We therefore
count pairs \((P,v)\), where \(P\) ranges over all monic polynomials of
degree \(p\) and \(v\) solves \eqref{eq:main}:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
\item a \emph{rational-exponential pair} has \(v\) rational in \(e^z\),
with one pole modulo \(2\pi i\ZZ\), placed at \(i\pi\), so that the
profile is smooth on the real axis, and \(v\to0\) as \(e^z\to0\), that
is, as \(z\to-\infty\) along the real axis;
\item an \emph{elliptic pair} has \(v\) elliptic on a given lattice,
with its only pole at zero.
\end{itemize}
A wave read in the opposite direction, \(z\mapsto-z\), is counted
separately. At the same rate it is a wave of the mirror-image equation,
in which the terms of one parity have changed sign, and restoring the
normalization gives again a pair, the \emph{mirror image} of the first.
For a fixed equation it is the same wave, read with the rate
\(-\kappa\).

One more convention explains a factor two below. The profile equation
has two constant solutions, and a wave can be measured from either of
them. The change is a Galilean shift: it changes the speed relative to
the background, and with it \(P\), but not the wave
(Section~\ref{sec:setting}). A rational-exponential wave is measured
from its limit as \(e^z\to0\). An elliptic wave has no such limit, and
both choices are counted, so every elliptic wave appears in two pairs.

\paragraph{The answer.}
Four numbers appear:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 N_p&=\binom{2p-1}{p-1}&&\text{rational-exponential pairs},\\
 S_p&=\binom{2p-2}{p-2}
     &&\text{the pulses among them; the other \(N_p-S_p\) are fronts},\\
 M_p&=2S_p&&\text{elliptic pairs on a generic lattice},\\
 F_p&=\binom{p-1}{\lfloor(p-1)/2\rfloor}
     &&\text{rational-exponential pairs of the pure subfamily of order
       \(p\)}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
We call \(2p-1\) the \emph{index} of the order \(p\). Our main result
establishes these numbers whenever the index is a prime number:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}
\item there are exactly \(N_p\) rational-exponential pairs, and they are
all distinct (Theorem~\ref{thm:prime});
\item exactly \(S_p\) of them are pulses, and the others are fronts
(Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints});
\item a generic lattice carries exactly \(M_p=2S_p\) elliptic pairs, and
they are all distinct (Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count}); generic means
every lattice except those with finitely many values of the invariant
\(j(\Lambda)\), which at \(p=3\) are \(j=0\) and \(-300\);
\item exactly \(F_p\) of the rational-exponential pairs belong to the
pure subfamily of order \(p\), and they are the pairs that coincide with
their mirror image. Those of a purely dispersive equation are pulses,
and those of a purely dissipative one are fronts. For even \(p\),
\(2F_p\) of the elliptic pairs belong to purely dispersive equations
(Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}, Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric}).
\end{itemize}
The index is prime for \(p=2,3,4,6,7,9,10,12,\ldots\). At the three
remaining orders up to twelve, \(p=5\), \(8\) and \(11\), the same
statements are established by exact computer calculations
(Corollary~\ref{cor:twelve}, Proposition~\ref{prop:general}(iii) and
Appendix~\ref{app:verification}). We call the orders with a prime index,
together with all \(p\le12\), the \emph{covered} orders; they include
every named equation of Table~\ref{tab:named}. We conjecture the same
statements in every order (Conjecture~\ref{conj:count}). At a prime
index the first statement has also been verified formally, in Lean
(Remark~\ref{rem:lean}).

The names and some physical sources: Benney--Lin
\citep{Benney1966,Lin1974}; Kuramoto--Sivashinsky with dispersion, also
called the generalized Kuramoto--Sivashinsky, Benney or KdV--KS equation
\citep{KawaharaToh1988}; Nikolaevskiy \citep{Nikolaevskii1989}, with
dispersion \citep{KudryashovMigita2007,SimbawaMatthewsCox2010}; long
waves in a liquid with gas bubbles
\citep{KudryashovSinelshchikov2010,KudryashovSinelshchikovFourthOrder2010}
and in a viscoelastic tube
\citep{KudryashovChernyavskii2006,KudryashovSinelshchikovChernyavsky2008},
where the mixed fifth-order equation of the Benney--Lin row was also
derived \citep{KudryashovSinelshchikov2011} and studied numerically
\citep{KudryashovRyabovSinelshchikov2011}. The rows with \(N_p\) and
\(M_p\) are full families, and a full family contains the pure subfamily
of the same \(p\). The small entries are familiar. The three pairs of
KdV--Burgers are the KdV soliton and the KdV--Burgers front, read in its
two directions. The two elliptic pairs of KdV are the cnoidal wave on
its two backgrounds. The eight elliptic pairs at \(p=3\) are
Kudryashov's elliptic solution: on a given lattice it occurs four times,
always with \(\sigma=\pm4\), and each time on two backgrounds
(Section~\ref{sec:ks}). The two entries \(0\) say that these purely
dissipative equations have no elliptic pair on a generic lattice
(Section~\ref{sec:reflection}); at \(p=3\) they have one only when
\(g_2=0\). The six pairs of the purely dissipative family with \(p=5\)
are the six solitary waves of \citet{KudryashovDemina2007}, four of them
real \citep{KudryashovMigita2007} (Section~\ref{sec:p5}).

The counts are over \(\CC\), but many pairs have real coefficients and
give smooth bounded waves of real equations: eight of the ten at \(p=3\)
and \(21\) of the \(35\) at \(p=4\) (Table~\ref{tab:ks},
Section~\ref{sec:kawahara}). A pair with non-real coefficients at unit
rate can still belong to a real equation, at an imaginary rate; the wave
is then periodic in \(x\) and has poles on the real axis
(Section~\ref{sec:ks-rational}). \emph{Distinct} carries content: like a
root of a polynomial, a solution of a polynomial system can be multiple,
and on special lattices elliptic pairs do merge in this way
(Section~\ref{sec:ks}). Rational waves are not counted: at the covered
orders the only one with a single pole is \(v=c_\ast z^{-p}\), for
\(P=D^p\) \citep{Kudryashov2004}, with \(c_\ast\) as in
\eqref{eq:leadingpole}. The general enumeration has no precedent that we
know of. Its special cases at \(p=3\), in the Kawahara family
\citep{Kudryashov1989,DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010} and in the
Nikolaevskiy family \citep{KudryashovDemina2007} are in the literature
(Sections~\ref{sec:ks-rational}, \ref{sec:examples}). What is new is a
uniform explanation across orders of why the lists are complete and
how long they are. We do not discuss stability, and treat only the
quadratic nonlinearity \(uu_x\).

\paragraph{A single equation.}
The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:named} are totals over all coefficient
values of a family. Rescaling \(x\) moves an equation along a curve in
the space of coefficient ratios, and each pair marks one curve
(Section~\ref{sec:fixed}). The equations with a one-pole
rational-exponential wave are those on these curves; whenever \(2p-1\)
is prime, and for every \(p\le573\), the curves are finite in number
(Corollary~\ref{cor:solvable}). This is the precise sense in which the
waves are exceptional for \(p\ge3\); at \(p=3\) the curves are
\(b_2^2=\sigma^2b_1b_3\) with the four values of \(\sigma\). In a family
with a single ratio (KdV--Burgers, Kawahara, Kuramoto--Sivashinsky
without dispersion) a possibly complex rescaling carries each equation
with a nonzero ratio to any other, so each has the KdV--Burgers front,
respectively all \(F_4=3\) or \(F_3=2\) wave shapes of its family. An
equation without lower-order terms, such as KdV, has each of its waves
at every rate.

For equations of even order the classification gives more: at the same
orders a generic such equation (outside a proper algebraic subset of the
coefficients) has no nonconstant meromorphic travelling wave at all
(Corollary~\ref{cor:generic}; Remark~\ref{rem:ks-curves} for \(p=3\)).
It may well have travelling waves; but none of them is meromorphic. The
counts also apply to the many other equations, with mixed derivatives or
in several space variables, that have the same profile equation. They
are a checklist for the output of expansions in powers of \(\tanh\) or a
Jacobi function: always for the equations of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete},
otherwise for outputs with one pole per period.

\paragraph{When these are all the meromorphic waves.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} is the classification stated above, for
equations of even order and purely dispersive ones. The proof rests on
Eremenko's theorem \citep{Eremenko2006}, which requires finitely many
Laurent expansions at a pole. For odd \(p\) the expansion is unique. For
a purely dispersive equation one Laurent coefficient is free, a
resonance; but the equation conserves an energy, and fixing the energy
fixes that coefficient. By the same uniqueness, every wave of a pure
subfamily is symmetric about its poles, or antisymmetric up to a
constant (Corollary~\ref{cor:symmetric-waves}). The theorem is a uniform
version of results that exist in pieces
(Section~\ref{sec:completeness}): odd \(p\) is contained in
\citet[Theorem~1]{YuanLiQi2014}, and the energy device is that of
\citet[Lemmas~3--4]{EremenkoLiaoNg2009}. The polynomial systems for the
waves are instances of the principal-part construction of
\citet{DeminaKudryashov2011,DeminaKudryashovElliptic2011}. Mixed
equations of odd order cannot be included: the profile equation of
KdV--Burgers is also that of the Fisher equation, and at the speed of
Ablowitz and Zeppetella it has meromorphic solutions of none of these
kinds (Remark~\ref{rem:fisher}).

\paragraph{Every wave has a label.}
Here is where the binomial coefficient \(N_p\) comes from. Put
\(Q=1/(1+e^{-z})=(1+\tanh(z/2))/2\). Every rational-exponential pair has
a profile of the form \(v_A=s_pA(D)Q\), where
\(s_p=2(2p-1)!/((p-1)!)^2\) is a fixed scale (\(s_2=12\), \(s_3=60\))
and \(A\) is a monic polynomial of degree \(p-1\); this is a polynomial
in \(\tanh(z/2)\) written in a convenient basis. For
\(\operatorname{Re}z<0\) we have \(Q=e^z-e^{2z}+e^{3z}-\cdots\), and
\(D\) multiplies \(e^{nz}\) by \(n\). Hence the coefficient of
\(e^{nz}\) is \(\pm s_pA(n)\) in \(v_A\) and \(\pm s_pP(n)A(n)\) in
\(P(D)v_A\), while in \(v_A^2\) it is \(\pm s_p^2S_A(n)\) with the
convolution sum
\[
 S_A(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}A(j)A(n-j).
\]
These sums are the values of a polynomial \(S_A\) of degree \(2p-1\),
and \eqref{eq:main} holds exactly when \(2P(n)A(n)=s_pS_A(n)\) for all
\(n\). So the pair exists if and only if the polynomial \(A\) divides
the polynomial \(S_A\), and then \(P\) is the quotient, up to the
constant factor (Proposition~\ref{prop:divisibility}).

The smallest case shows what happens. For \(p=2\) and \(A=\rho+a\),
\[
 S_A(\rho)=(\rho-1)\Bigl(\frac{\rho(\rho+1)}6+a\rho+a^2\Bigr),
\]
which is divisible by \(\rho+a\) exactly when \(a(a-1)(a+1)=0\). The
solution \(a=0\) is the KdV soliton, and \(a=\pm1\) are the KdV--Burgers
front read in its two directions. Now reduce modulo \(3=2p-1\). Whatever
the value of \(a\),
\[
 3S_A(\rho)\equiv-(\rho^3-\rho)=-\rho(\rho-1)(\rho+1)\pmod3,
\]
so modulo \(3\) the divisor \(A\) must be one of the three factors
\(\rho\), \(\rho-1\), \(\rho+1\). Each of them comes from exactly one
solution, and the root of \(A\) modulo \(3\) serves as a label:
\(\{0\}\) for the soliton, \(\{2\}\) for the front \(a=1\), that is, the
root \(-1\equiv2\), and \(\{1\}\) for the front \(a=-1\).

The same happens whenever \(\ell=2p-1\) is prime. The polynomial \(S_A\)
is a combination of the binomial coefficients \(\binom{\rho-1}{k}\), and
only the last of them, with \(k=\ell\), has \(\ell\) in its denominator.
For this reason \(\ell S_A\) reduces modulo \(\ell\) to
\(\pm(\rho^\ell-\rho)\), whatever \(A\) is, and the condition becomes
that \(A\) divides \(\rho^\ell-\rho\) modulo \(\ell\). Now
\(\rho^\ell-\rho\) is the product of \(\rho-s\) over all residues \(s\)
modulo \(\ell\). Its monic divisors of degree \(p-1\) are therefore
given by the \((p-1)\)-element subsets \(L\) of the integers modulo
\(\ell\), and there are \(\binom{\ell}{p-1}=N_p\) of them. We prove that
every pair can be reduced modulo \(\ell\), although its coefficients are
in general algebraic numbers and not integers, and that every divisor
comes from exactly one pair. An algebraic number is reduced through an
equation that it satisfies: \(i\) is \(2\) or \(3\) modulo \(5\),
because \(2^2\equiv3^2\equiv-1\). For instance \(A=\rho^2+1/11\), the
Kuramoto--Tsuzuki wave, reduces to \(\rho^2+1=(\rho-2)(\rho-3)\) modulo
\(5\), and has the label \(\{2,3\}\).

So each pair has a label \(L\), and the labels carry information. The
pair is a pulse exactly when \(L\) contains \(0\). At \(p=3\) the ten
classical waves correspond to the ten two-element subsets of
\(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\) (Table~\ref{tab:ks}), and the four pulses are the
subsets that contain \(0\). The mirror image of the pair with label
\(L\) has label \(-L\). The pairs with \(L=-L\) coincide with their
mirror image, and they are exactly the pairs of the two pure
subfamilies. There are \(F_p\) such subsets.

The elliptic count comes from the pulses. When one period of the lattice
tends to infinity, an elliptic pair becomes a pulse, measured from one
of its two constant backgrounds. We show that every pulse, on either
background, arises in this way exactly once, and this gives
\(M_p=2S_p\).

\paragraph{Other orders.}
The number \(N_p\) is also the largest number of isolated solutions,
counted with multiplicity, that matching equations of this shape can
have (Proposition~\ref{prop:bezout}). It is attained if no solution lies
at infinity, which is rigidity, and the solutions are distinct if all of
them are simple. These are the two facts that are not yet proved at
every order. For waves, rigidity says that \(c_\ast z^{-p}\) is the only
rational wave with a single pole (Lemma~\ref{lem:laplace}). It is proved
when \(2p-1\) is a prime or a prime power and established by exact
computations for \(p\le573\); simplicity is known only at the covered
orders (Section~\ref{sec:further} and a companion note).

The counts come first (Sections~\ref{sec:counting},
\ref{sec:elliptic-counting}): they do not use the classification
(Section~\ref{sec:completeness}). Their proofs

in Sections~\ref{sec:reflection} and~\ref{sec:fixed} that follow the
steps of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count}.
