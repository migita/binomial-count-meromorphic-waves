\begin{abstract}
We study meromorphic travelling waves of
\(u_t+uu_x+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_j\partial_x^{j+1}u=0\), a
constant-coefficient family containing KdV--Burgers,
Kuramoto--Sivashinsky and Kawahara. After integration and
normalization, the profiles satisfy \(P(D)v+v^2/2=0\), where
\(D=\mathrm d/\mathrm dz\) and \(P\) is monic of degree \(p\ge2\) with
complex coefficients.

For evolution equations of even order (\(p\) odd), and for purely
dispersive equations (only odd-order derivatives), we give a complete
algebraic description of all nonconstant globally meromorphic
travelling waves: they are rational, rational in an exponential, or
elliptic, with one pole per period.

One-pole waves of these three kinds are exceptional: for \(p\ge3\) a
generic equation of the family has none. We count the exceptions as
pairs \((P,v)\), over the whole family. Rescaling \(x\) changes \(P\)
and the period of \(v\) together, so the period is held fixed. There
are \(\binom{2p-1}{p-1}\) such pairs with \(v\) rational in an
exponential of given rate, and \(2\binom{2p-2}{p-2}\) with \(v\)
elliptic on a given generic lattice. We also count how many of these
belong to the purely dispersive subfamily (\(p\) even) and to the
purely dissipative one (only even-order derivatives in the linear
part, \(p\) odd). The subfamily present at a given \(p\) contains
\(F_p=\binom{p-1}{\lfloor(p-1)/2\rfloor}\) of the rational-exponential
pairs: all solitary pulses in the dispersive case, all fronts in the
dissipative case.
The purely dispersive subfamily also contains \(2F_p\) of the elliptic
pairs.

When \(2p-1\) is prime, these statements are proved and all pairs are
distinct. Exact computer certificates establish the counts, with
multiplicities, for \(2\le p\le73\). We conjecture the same in every
order.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.}
Meromorphic travelling waves; dispersive and dissipative equations;
elliptic functions; polynomial convolution; algebraic multiplicity.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Exact travelling waves of nonlinear evolution equations are usually
found one equation at a time, and usually only for special values of
the coefficients. The generalized Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation is a
classical example. It has nonconstant travelling waves
that are polynomials in a hyperbolic tangent at exactly four values of
its squared dimensionless dispersion parameter. Kuramoto and Tsuzuki
found the dispersionless one \citep{KuramotoTsuzuki1976}, Kudryashov the
other three \citep{Kudryashov1988,Kudryashov1990}, and Eremenko proved
that, together with Kudryashov's elliptic solution and a single rational
one, they exhaust the nonconstant meromorphic travelling waves
\citep{Eremenko2006}.
The classical tables list six cases at these four values
\citep{KudryashovZargaryan1996,ConteMusette2009}. We count a wave and
its mirror image separately. The two waves of the dispersionless
equation are their own mirror images and the other four are not, so the
list has ten members. This paper shows that ten is \(\binom52\): the
classical list is the case \(p=3\) of a binomial enumeration, proved
for infinitely many \(p\) and conjectured for all.

We consider the evolution equations
\[
 u_t+uu_x+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_j\partial_x^{j+1}u=0,\qquad b_p\ne0,
\]
which contain KdV--Burgers (\(p=2\)), Kuramoto--Sivashinsky (\(p=3\))
and Kawahara (\(p=4\)), the sixth-order turbulence models of
\citet{KudryashovMigita2007,SimbawaMatthewsCox2010}, and the quadratic
long-wave reductions of \citet{KudryashovSinelshchikovChernyavsky2008}.
A travelling wave \(u=w(x-ct)\) satisfies, after one integration,
\(\sum_jb_jw^{(j)}-cw+w^2/2+K=0\). Shifting \(w\) by a constant
solution and normalizing the leading coefficient gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 E_P(v):=P(D)v+\frac12v^2=0,\qquad
 P(\rho)=\rho^p+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}a_j\rho^j,\qquad p\ge2,
\end{equation}
with \(D=\mathrm d/\mathrm dz\) and complex coefficients. The speed,
the integration constant and the background are absorbed into
\(a_0\) (Section~\ref{sec:setting}).

Throughout, \(p\) is the order of the profile equation
\eqref{eq:main}. The evolution equation has order \(p+1\):
Kuramoto--Sivashinsky is a fourth-order equation and has \(p=3\), and
Kawahara is a fifth-order equation and has \(p=4\). Phrases such as
``order three'' or ``in every order'' always refer to \(p\). The order
of the evolution equation appears only in customary names, such as the
seventh-order KdV equation, which has \(p=6\). We call \(2p-1\) the
\emph{index} of the order \(p\).

Two subfamilies play a special role. In the evolution equation,
derivatives of odd order are dispersive terms and derivatives of even
order are dissipative terms. We call an equation \emph{purely
dispersive} if it contains only odd-order derivatives, as the KdV and
Kawahara equations do; then \(p\) is even and \(P\) is an even
polynomial. We call it \emph{purely dissipative} if its linear part
contains only even-order derivatives, as for the
Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation without dispersion; then \(p\) is odd
and \(P-P(0)\) is an odd polynomial. All other equations, such as
KdV--Burgers, are \emph{mixed}. Both pure subfamilies have a
reflection symmetry, which is described in Section~\ref{sec:setting}.

\paragraph{Classification.}
Eremenko's theorem states that an autonomous algebraic differential
equation with a single monomial of top degree and finitely many Laurent
expansions at a pole has only three kinds of nonconstant meromorphic
solutions: rational, rational in an exponential, and elliptic
\citep{Eremenko2006}. Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} verifies these
hypotheses for \eqref{eq:main} in two cases. The first is \(p\) odd,
that is, every evolution equation of even order: there the Laurent
expansion at a pole is unique. The second is the purely dispersive
case: there one Laurent coefficient is free, but \eqref{eq:main} is
invariant under \(z\mapsto-z\) and conserves an energy, and fixing the
energy fixes that coefficient. In both cases every nonconstant
meromorphic travelling wave is of one of the three kinds and has one
pole per period. For a fixed operator \(P\) there is at most one such
profile up to translation when \(p\) is odd, and at most one on each
energy level when the equation is purely dispersive. Mixed operators
with \(p\) even, such as that of KdV--Burgers, are not covered, and
cannot be: Section~\ref{sec:open} recalls an example with \(p=2\) that
has other meromorphic waves. Section~\ref{sec:systems} writes
each of the three kinds as a finite polynomial system, following the
principal-part construction of
\citet{DeminaKudryashov2011,DeminaKudryashovElliptic2011}.

\paragraph{The count.}
One-pole waves of these three kinds are exceptional. In every order
\(p\ge3\) for which the count below is established, a generic equation
of the family has none of them (Section~\ref{sec:fixed}). Here and
below, generic means outside a proper algebraic subset of the
coefficients, or of the lattices. It refers to the full family: a pure
subfamily of low order, such as the KdV or Kawahara equations, can lie
on the exceptional set. We count the exceptions.

Translations and changes of length scale produce continuous families of
equivalent descriptions, so before asking for a finite count we fix
these freedoms. For a rational-exponential wave we prescribe its
primitive period, or equivalently its exponential rate. For an elliptic
wave we prescribe its full period lattice, including its scale. We then
place the pole at a fixed point. The coefficients of \(P\) still vary:
we count which normalized equations admit such a wave, together with
the wave itself.
Precisely, a rational-exponential pair \((P,v)\) has \(v\) rational in
\(e^z\), with one pole modulo \(2\pi i\ZZ\), placed at \(i\pi\), and
\(v\to0\) as \(e^z\to0\). An elliptic pair has \(v\) elliptic on a
fixed nonsingular lattice, with its only pole at zero; each elliptic
wave is then counted on both of its constant backgrounds, which is the
factor two below. In both cases
\(P\) ranges over all monic polynomials of degree \(p\).
The answers are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 N_p&=\binom{2p-1}{p-1}
     &&\text{rational-exponential pairs},\\
 M_p&=2\binom{2p-2}{p-2}
     &&\text{elliptic pairs on a generic lattice}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The pairs are the solutions of a polynomial system with as many
equations as unknowns, and a solution can be simple or multiple, like a
root of a polynomial.
Whenever \(2p-1\) is prime, both formulas hold and every pair has
multiplicity one, so there are exactly \(N_p\) distinct pairs, and
exactly \(M_p\) on a generic lattice
(Theorems~\ref{thm:prime} and~\ref{thm:elliptic-count}).
For every \(2\le p\le73\), the formulas hold when each pair is counted
with its multiplicity (Section~\ref{sec:further}); the number of
distinct pairs is then at most \(N_p\) or \(M_p\), with equality when
no pair is multiple. On special lattices elliptic
pairs do merge, and Section~\ref{sec:ks} determines where this happens
at order three.
At \(p=5\), where \(2p-1=9\) is the first composite index, a separate
exact computation shows that all \(126\) rational-exponential pairs
have multiplicity one.
We conjecture the same counting and multiplicity statements in every
order (Conjecture~\ref{conj:count}). The counts are over \(\CC\) and
range over all monic \(P\), mixed operators included. The
classification is used only to know that, where it applies, there are
no other meromorphic waves. Rational waves are not counted: under the
rigidity condition of Section~\ref{sec:counting} the only one is
\(v=c_\ast z^{-p}\), for \(P=D^p\).

\paragraph{Purely dispersive and purely dissipative equations.}
Reading a wave in the opposite direction, \(z\mapsto-z\), and restoring
the normalization sends pairs to pairs. The pairs that coincide with
their mirror image are exactly those whose equation is purely
dispersive, for even \(p\), or purely dissipative, for odd \(p\)
(Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}). Their number is
\[
 F_p=\binom{p-1}{\lfloor(p-1)/2\rfloor},
\]
with the same status as \(N_p\): it is proved, with all pairs distinct,
when \(2p-1\) is prime, and as a count with multiplicity for
\(2\le p\le73\). The purely dispersive equations also account for
\(2F_p\) of the elliptic pairs on a generic lattice
(Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric}).

The two subfamilies carry different kinds of rational-exponential
waves. Those of a purely dispersive equation are all pulses, which
return to the same constant at both ends; this holds for every even
\(p\). Those of a purely dissipative equation are all fronts, which
connect two different constants. This second statement is proved for
every \(p\) at which all \(N_p\) pairs are distinct, in particular when
\(2p-1\) is prime, and for waves with real coefficients at every odd
\(p\). For example, the Kawahara family has three rational-exponential
pairs and six elliptic pairs on a generic lattice, which are \(F_4\)
and \(2F_4\); the seventh-order KdV family has ten pairs, two of them
real; and the six pairs of the purely dissipative family with \(p=5\)
contain the four kink solutions of \citet{KudryashovMigita2007}
(Section~\ref{sec:examples}).

\paragraph{A single equation.}
A count across the family is not a count for each individual equation.
To recover the waves of a given equation, we restore the length scale
and retain only the normalized pairs whose coefficients match it.
Changing the scale traces a curve in the space of coefficients. Thus,
in the orders covered by the count, the equations with a one-pole
rational-exponential wave lie on finitely many such scaling curves. If at least one lower-derivative coefficient
is nonzero, only finitely many wave shapes remain after translations
and Galilean shifts are identified. The scale-invariant case is
different: the KdV equation, for example, retains its family of
solitons of different widths. Section~\ref{sec:fixed} gives the precise
criterion, recovers the four familiar Kuramoto--Sivashinsky coefficient
curves, and shows that for odd \(p\ge3\) in the settled range a generic
equation has no nonconstant meromorphic travelling wave at all.

\paragraph{Method.}
Every rational-exponential pair has a profile of the form
\(v_A=s_pA(D)Q\), where \(Q=1/(1+e^{-z})\), \(s_p\) is a fixed scale
and \(A\) is a monic polynomial of degree \(p-1\). The pair solves
\eqref{eq:main} if and only if \(A\) divides its own discrete
self-convolution \(S_A(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}A(j)A(n-j)\)
(Proposition~\ref{prop:divisibility}). When \(\ell=2p-1\) is prime,
this condition becomes \(A\mid\rho^\ell-\rho\) modulo \(\ell\), so its
solutions are labelled by the \((p-1)\)-element subsets \(S\) of
\(\FF_\ell\). This is where the binomial coefficient comes from. Each
label lifts to exactly one solution, and the reflection acts on the
labels by \(S\mapsto-S\), which gives \(F_p\). The elliptic count uses
two limits of the lattice. When both periods become infinite, the
elliptic problem becomes a rational one, and this limit gives the count
with multiplicity on every nonsingular lattice. When one period becomes
infinite, it becomes the pulse case of the exponential problem, with
two choices of background, and this limit shows that the pairs have
multiplicity one on a generic lattice.

Two ingredients are not yet proved in every order. The first is a
rigidity statement: the only rational solution of \eqref{eq:main} with
one pole that vanishes at infinity is \(c_\ast z^{-p}\), with
\(P=D^p\). Equivalently, a monic polynomial \(A\) of degree \(p-1\)
that divides its continuous self-convolution
\(\int_0^\rho A(t)A(\rho-t)\dd t\) is \(\rho^{p-1}\). Rigidity gives all
counts with multiplicity. It is proved when \(2p-1\) is a prime or a
prime power, and certified by exact arithmetic for \(p\le73\). The
second is that every rational-exponential pair has multiplicity one,
which makes the pairs distinct. It is proved when \(2p-1\) is prime
and, by exact computation, at \(p=5\). Both are conjectured in general
(Conjecture~\ref{conj:count}).

\paragraph{Relation to earlier work.}
The classification theorem is a uniform version of results that exist
in pieces. The case of odd \(p\) is contained in
\citet[Theorem~1]{YuanLiQi2014}, and the energy device for even \(P\) is
that of \citet[Lemmas~3--4]{EremenkoLiaoNg2009}, which has been applied
to the Kawahara, generalized Bretherton and seventh-order KdV
equations; Section~\ref{sec:completeness} gives the detailed
attributions. The polynomial systems are instances of the
principal-part construction of
\citet{DeminaKudryashov2011,DeminaKudryashovElliptic2011}.
The general enumeration has no precedent that we know of; its special
cases at \(p=3\) and in the Kawahara family are classical, as
the examples make explicit.

\paragraph{Organization.}
Section~\ref{sec:setting} fixes the normalizations and the vocabulary.
Sections~\ref{sec:completeness} and~\ref{sec:systems} contain the
classification and the three polynomial systems.
Sections~\ref{sec:counting} and~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting} prove the
two counts, Section~\ref{sec:further} records which orders are
settled, and Section~\ref{sec:fixed} reads the count for a single
equation. Section~\ref{sec:examples} works out the lowest orders and
the two pure subfamilies, including the lattices on which elliptic
pairs merge. Section~\ref{sec:open} collects the open questions, and
Appendix~\ref{app:verification} describes the exact verification and
the ancillary material. All counts are over \(\CC\); the examples
identify the real profiles, and stability is not discussed.
