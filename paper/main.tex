\begin{abstract}
We study meromorphic travelling waves of
\(u_t+uu_x+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_j\partial_x^{j+1}u=0\), a
constant-coefficient family containing KdV--Burgers,
Kuramoto--Sivashinsky and Kawahara. After integration and
normalization, the profiles satisfy \(P(D)v+v^2/2=0\), where
\(D=\mathrm d/\mathrm dz\) and \(P\) is monic of degree \(p\ge2\) with
complex coefficients.

For evolution equations of even order (\(p\) odd), and for purely
dispersive equations (only odd-order derivatives), we give a complete
algebraic description of all nonconstant globally meromorphic
travelling waves: they are rational, rational in an exponential, or
elliptic, with one pole per period.

One-pole waves of these three kinds are exceptional: for \(p\ge3\) a
generic equation of the family has none. We count the exceptions as
pairs \((P,v)\), over the whole family: there are
\(\binom{2p-1}{p-1}\) with \(v\) rational in an exponential of given
rate, and \(2\binom{2p-2}{p-2}\) with \(v\) elliptic on a given generic
lattice. We also count the solitary pulses of the purely dispersive
subfamily and the fronts of the purely dissipative one (only even-order
derivatives in the linear part).

When \(2p-1\) is prime, these statements are proved and all pairs are
distinct. We conjecture them in every order.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.}
Meromorphic travelling waves; dispersive and dissipative equations;
elliptic functions; polynomial convolution; algebraic multiplicity.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Exact travelling waves of nonlinear evolution equations are usually
found one equation at a time, and usually only for special values of
the coefficients. The Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation with dispersion,
\(u_t+uu_x+u_{xx}+\sigma u_{xxx}+u_{xxxx}=0\), is a classical example.
It has nonconstant travelling waves that are polynomials in a
hyperbolic tangent for exactly four values of the dispersion
coefficient, up to its sign: \(\sigma=0\), \(4\), \(12/\sqrt{47}\) and
\(16/\sqrt{73}\). Kuramoto and Tsuzuki found the wave of the
dispersionless equation \citep{KuramotoTsuzuki1976}, Kudryashov the
others
\citep{Kudryashov1988,Kudryashov1989,Kudryashov1990,Kudryashov1991},
and Eremenko proved that, together with Kudryashov's elliptic solution
and a single rational one, they exhaust the nonconstant meromorphic
travelling waves \citep{Eremenko2006}.
The classical tables list six cases at these four values
\citep{KudryashovZargaryan1996,ConteMusette2009}: two at \(\sigma=0\),
two at \(\sigma=\pm4\), and one at each of the other two values. The
four values already appear together in
\citet[Eq.~(39)]{Kudryashov1989}. The tables do not distinguish a wave
from its mirror image, which solves the equation with the opposite
sign of \(\sigma\). If the two are counted
separately, the two cases with \(\sigma=0\) are their own mirror images
and the other four are not, so the list has \(2+2\cdot4=10\) members.
This paper shows that ten is \(\binom52\): the classical list is the
case \(p=3\) of a binomial enumeration, proved for infinitely many
\(p\) and conjectured for all.

We consider the evolution equations
\[
 u_t+uu_x+\sum_{j=1}^{p}b_j\partial_x^{j+1}u=0,\qquad b_p\ne0,
\]
which contain the KdV--Burgers (\(p=2\)), Kuramoto--Sivashinsky
(\(p=3\)), Kawahara and Benney--Lin (\(p=4\)) and Nikolaevskiy
(\(p=5\)) equations; Table~\ref{tab:named} lists the named members with
\(p\le6\). Kudryashov studied this family as a whole and found exact
waves of its KdV--Burgers, Kuramoto--Sivashinsky and Kawahara members
\citep{Kudryashov1989}.
A travelling wave \(u=w(x-ct)\) satisfies, after one integration,
\(\sum_jb_jw^{(j)}-cw+w^2/2+K=0\). Shifting \(w\) by a constant
solution and normalizing the leading coefficient gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main}
 P(D)v+\frac12v^2=0,\qquad
 P(\rho)=\rho^p+\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}a_j\rho^j,\qquad p\ge2,
\end{equation}
with \(D=\mathrm d/\mathrm dz\) and complex coefficients. The
coefficients \(a_1,\ldots,a_{p-1}\) are those of the evolution
equation, up to a rescaling of \(x\), and the speed, the integration
constant and the background are absorbed into \(a_0\)
(Section~\ref{sec:setting}).
Throughout, \(p\) is the order of the profile equation \eqref{eq:main},
which is one less than the order of the evolution equation:
Kuramoto--Sivashinsky is a fourth-order equation and has \(p=3\),
Kawahara has \(p=4\), and the seventh-order KdV equation has \(p=6\).
Phrases such as ``order three'' or ``in every order'' always refer to
\(p\).

In the evolution equation, derivatives of odd order are dispersive
terms and derivatives of even order are dissipative terms (in this sign
convention a positive coefficient of \(u_{xx}\) is gain, so dissipative
includes gain). We call an equation \emph{purely dispersive} if it
contains only odd-order derivatives, as the KdV and Kawahara equations
do; then \(p\) is even and \(P\) is an even polynomial. We call it
\emph{purely dissipative} if its linear part contains only even-order
derivatives, as for the Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation without
dispersion; then \(p\) is odd and \(P-P(0)\) is an odd polynomial. All
other equations, such as KdV--Burgers, are \emph{mixed}. The first two
classes are \emph{the two pure subfamilies}; each order \(p\) has one
of them, according to its parity.

\paragraph{The waves.}
A travelling wave is \emph{meromorphic} if its profile is a
single-valued meromorphic function of the complex variable \(z\). The
familiar exact solutions are of this kind: a polynomial in
\(\tanh\) or \(\operatorname{sech}\) is a rational function of an
exponential \(e^{\kappa z}\), a cnoidal wave is an elliptic function,
and rational functions of \(z\) also occur. These are the three kinds
of wave in this paper. We consider those with \emph{one pole per
period}: all the poles of the profile are translates of a single pole
by its periods. For a wave of \(\tanh\) type the period in question is
the imaginary period \(2\pi i/\kappa\) of \(e^{\kappa z}\), and the
rate \(\kappa\) measures the width of the wave. Such a wave tends to a
constant at each end (Section~\ref{sec:systems}); it is a \emph{pulse}
if it returns to the same constant, and a \emph{front} if it connects
two different constants.

For every equation of even order, and for every purely dispersive
equation, these are all the meromorphic travelling waves that exist
(Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}). For the remaining equations, the mixed
ones of odd order such as KdV--Burgers, other meromorphic waves can
occur; there we count the one-pole waves without claiming more. These
equations are the rows marked \(\dagger\) in Table~\ref{tab:named}.

\paragraph{The question.}
For \(p\ge3\) such waves are exceptional: an equation of the family has
one only if its coefficients satisfy algebraic relations, as the four
values of \(\sigma\) illustrate. This suggests the right question. We
do not ask how many waves a given equation has, but how many pairs of
an equation and a wave there are in the whole family.

To make this question finite, two continuous freedoms must be removed.
A translate of a wave is again a wave, so we place the pole at a fixed
point. Rescaling \(x\) changes the width of a wave and the coefficients
of its equation together, so we fix the width: for a wave that is
rational in an exponential we prescribe the rate \(\kappa=1\), and for
an elliptic wave we prescribe both of its periods, that is, its period
lattice. The coefficients of the equation remain free. We therefore
count pairs \((P,v)\), where \(P\) ranges over all monic polynomials of
degree \(p\) and \(v\) solves \eqref{eq:main}:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item a \emph{rational-exponential pair} has \(v\) rational in
\(e^z\), with one pole modulo \(2\pi i\ZZ\), placed at \(i\pi\), so
that the profile is smooth on the real axis, and \(v\to0\) as
\(e^z\to0\), that is, as \(z\to-\infty\) along the real axis;
\item an \emph{elliptic pair} has \(v\) elliptic on a given lattice,
with its only pole at zero.
\end{itemize}
A wave read in the opposite direction, \(z\mapsto-z\), is counted
separately. At the same rate it is a wave of the mirror-image equation,
in which the terms of one parity have changed sign, and restoring the
normalization gives again a pair, the \emph{mirror image} of the first.
For a fixed equation it is the same wave, read with the rate
\(-\kappa\).

One more convention explains a factor two below. The profile equation
has two constant solutions, and a wave can be measured from either of
them. The change is a Galilean shift: it changes the speed relative to
the background, and with it \(P\), but not the wave
(Section~\ref{sec:setting}).
A rational-exponential wave is measured from its limit as \(e^z\to0\).
An elliptic wave has no such limit, and both choices are counted, so
every elliptic wave appears in two pairs.

\paragraph{The answer.}
Four numbers appear:
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 N_p&=\binom{2p-1}{p-1}
     &&\text{rational-exponential pairs},\\
 S_p&=\binom{2p-2}{p-2}
     &&\text{the pulses among them; the other \(N_p-S_p\) are fronts},\\
 M_p&=2S_p
     &&\text{elliptic pairs on a generic lattice},\\
 F_p&=\binom{p-1}{\lfloor(p-1)/2\rfloor}
     &&\text{rational-exponential pairs of the pure subfamily of order
       \(p\)}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
We call \(2p-1\) the \emph{index} of the order \(p\). Our main result
establishes these numbers whenever the index is a prime number:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item there are exactly \(N_p\) rational-exponential pairs, and they
are all distinct (Theorem~\ref{thm:prime});
\item exactly \(S_p\) of them are pulses, and the others are fronts
(Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints});
\item a generic lattice carries exactly \(M_p=2S_p\) elliptic pairs,
and they are all distinct (Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count}); generic
means every lattice except those with finitely many values of the
invariant \(j(\Lambda)\), which at \(p=3\) are \(j=0\) and \(-300\);
\item exactly \(F_p\) of the rational-exponential pairs belong to the
pure subfamily of order \(p\), and they are the pairs that coincide
with their mirror image. Those of a purely dispersive equation are
pulses, and those of a purely dissipative one are fronts. For even
\(p\), \(2F_p\) of the elliptic pairs belong to purely dispersive
equations (Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric},
Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric}).
\end{itemize}
The index is prime for \(p=2,3,4,6,7,9,10,12,\ldots\). At the three
remaining orders up to twelve, \(p=5\), \(8\) and \(11\), the same
statements are established by exact computer calculations
(Corollary~\ref{cor:twelve} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:general}(iii)). We call the orders with a prime
index, together with all \(p\le12\), the \emph{covered} orders; they
include every named equation of Table~\ref{tab:named}. We conjecture
the same statements in every order (Conjecture~\ref{conj:count}). At a
prime index the first statement has also been verified formally, in
Lean (Remark~\ref{rem:lean}).

The named equations of Table~\ref{tab:named} are the Benney--Lin
equation \citep{Benney1966,Lin1974}, the Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation
with
dispersion, also called the generalized Kuramoto--Sivashinsky, Benney
or KdV--KS equation \citep{KawaharaToh1988}, the Nikolaevskiy equation
\citep{Nikolaevskii1989} and its dispersive version
\citep{KudryashovMigita2007,SimbawaMatthewsCox2010}, the long-wave
equations for a liquid with gas bubbles
\citep{KudryashovSinelshchikov2010,KudryashovSinelshchikovFourthOrder2010}
and for flow in a viscoelastic tube
\citep{KudryashovChernyavskii2006,KudryashovSinelshchikovChernyavsky2008},
where the mixed fifth-order equation of the Benney--Lin row was also
derived \citep{KudryashovSinelshchikov2011} and studied numerically
\citep{KudryashovRyabovSinelshchikov2011}, and the equations already
mentioned. The rows with \(N_p\) and \(M_p\) are full families, and a
full family contains the pure subfamily of the same \(p\). The small
entries are familiar. The three pairs of KdV--Burgers are the KdV
soliton and the KdV--Burgers front, read in its two directions. The
two elliptic pairs of KdV are the cnoidal wave on its two backgrounds.
The eight elliptic pairs at \(p=3\) are Kudryashov's elliptic
solution: on a given lattice it occurs four times, always with
\(\sigma=\pm4\), and each time on two backgrounds
(Section~\ref{sec:ks}). The two entries \(0\) say that these purely
dissipative equations have no elliptic pair on a generic lattice
(Section~\ref{sec:reflection}); at \(p=3\) they have one only when
\(g_2=0\). The six pairs of the purely dissipative family with \(p=5\)
are the six solitary waves of \citet{KudryashovDemina2007}, four of
them real \citep{KudryashovMigita2007} (Section~\ref{sec:p5}).

Some remarks make precise what the numbers mean.
The counts are over \(\CC\) and range over all monic \(P\). Many of the
pairs have real coefficients and give smooth bounded waves of real
equations: eight of the ten at \(p=3\) (Table~\ref{tab:ks}), and \(21\)
of the \(35\) at \(p=4\) (Section~\ref{sec:kawahara}). A pair with
non-real coefficients at unit rate can still belong to a real equation,
at an imaginary rate; the wave is then periodic in \(x\) and has poles
on the real axis (Section~\ref{sec:ks-rational}).
The word \emph{distinct} carries content: a solution of a polynomial
system can be multiple, like a multiple root of a polynomial, and on
special lattices elliptic pairs do merge in this way
(Section~\ref{sec:ks}).
Rational waves are not counted: at the covered orders the only one
with a single pole is \(v=c_\ast z^{-p}\), for \(P=D^p\)
\citep{Kudryashov2004}; the constant \(c_\ast\) is given in
\eqref{eq:leadingpole}.
Finally, the waves of low order are classical. The general enumeration
has no precedent that we know of. Its special cases at \(p=3\), in the
Kawahara family \citep{Kudryashov1989,DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010} and
in the purely dissipative family with \(p=5\)
\citep{KudryashovDemina2007} are in the literature, as
Sections~\ref{sec:ks-rational} and~\ref{sec:examples} make explicit;
what is new is that the lists are complete, and how long they are. We
do not discuss stability, and we treat only the quadratic nonlinearity
\(uu_x\).

\paragraph{A single equation.}
The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:named} are totals over all coefficient
values of a family. An individual equation has such a wave only if its
coefficient ratios lie on special curves (Section~\ref{sec:fixed}).
The reason is that rescaling \(x\) moves an equation along a curve in
the space of coefficient ratios, and each pair marks one curve.
In a family with a single coefficient ratio (KdV--Burgers, Kawahara,
Kuramoto--Sivashinsky without dispersion) a rescaling carries every
equation with a nonzero ratio to every other one. Each of these
equations then has all the waves whose ratio is not zero: the
KdV--Burgers front, and all \(F_4=3\) and \(F_3=2\) wave shapes of the
other two families. The rescaling may be complex: in the last two
families a real one cannot change the sign of the ratio.
An equation without lower-order terms, such as KdV, is invariant under
rescaling and has each of its waves at every rate.

Whenever \(2p-1\) is prime, and for every \(p\le573\), the equations
with a one-pole rational-exponential wave are those on finitely many
curves, at most one for each pair and its mirror image
(Corollary~\ref{cor:solvable}). This is the precise sense in which the
waves are exceptional for \(p\ge3\). At \(p=3\) the curves are
\(b_2^2=\sigma^2b_1b_3\) with the four values of \(\sigma\).
For equations of even order the classification gives more: a generic
such equation has no nonconstant meromorphic travelling wave at all,
at the same orders (Corollary~\ref{cor:generic}, and the remark after
it for \(p=3\)); generic means outside a proper algebraic subset of the
coefficients. It may well have travelling waves; but none of them is
meromorphic.
Section~\ref{sec:fixed} also records two consequences for the way exact
travelling waves are found in practice. The counts apply to the many
other equations, with mixed derivatives or in several space variables,
that have the same profile equation. And they are a checklist for the
output of the expansion methods in powers of \(\tanh\) or of a Jacobi
function: always for the equations of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}, and
otherwise for outputs with one pole per period.

\paragraph{When these are all the meromorphic waves.}
For every equation of even order (\(p\) odd) and for every purely
dispersive equation, the one-pole waves of the three kinds are all the
nonconstant meromorphic travelling waves there are
(Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}). The proof rests on Eremenko's theorem
\citep{Eremenko2006}: an autonomous algebraic differential equation
has only these three kinds of meromorphic solutions if it has a single
monomial of top degree, here \(v^2/2\), and finitely many Laurent
expansions at a pole. For odd \(p\) the Laurent expansion at a pole is
unique. For a purely dispersive equation one Laurent coefficient is
free, a resonance; but the equation conserves an energy, and fixing the
energy fixes that coefficient.
For a given operator \(P\), which records the equation and the speed,
there is therefore at most one such profile up to translation when
\(p\) is odd, and at most one on each energy level when the equation is
purely dispersive. A by-product is that every wave of a pure subfamily
is symmetric about its poles, or antisymmetric up to a constant
(Corollary~\ref{cor:symmetric-waves}).
The theorem is a uniform version of results that exist in pieces: the
case of odd \(p\) is contained in \citet[Theorem~1]{YuanLiQi2014}, and
the energy device is that of \citet[Lemmas~3--4]{EremenkoLiaoNg2009};
Section~\ref{sec:completeness} gives the detailed attributions. The
polynomial systems for the three kinds of wave are instances of the
principal-part construction of
\citet{DeminaKudryashov2011,DeminaKudryashovElliptic2011}.
The mixed equations of odd order are not included, and cannot be: the
profile equation of KdV--Burgers is also that of the Fisher equation,
and at the speed of Ablowitz and Zeppetella it has meromorphic
solutions of none of the three kinds (Remark~\ref{rem:fisher}).

\paragraph{Every wave has a label.}
Here is where the binomial coefficient \(N_p\) comes from. Put
\(Q=1/(1+e^{-z})=(1+\tanh(z/2))/2\). Every rational-exponential pair
has a profile of the form \(v_A=s_pA(D)Q\), where
\(s_p=2(2p-1)!/((p-1)!)^2\) is a fixed scale (\(s_2=12\), \(s_3=60\))
and \(A\) is a monic polynomial of degree \(p-1\); this is a
polynomial in \(\tanh(z/2)\) written in a convenient basis. For
\(\operatorname{Re}z<0\) we have \(Q=e^z-e^{2z}+e^{3z}-\cdots\), and
\(D\) multiplies \(e^{nz}\) by \(n\). Hence the coefficient of
\(e^{nz}\) is \(\pm s_pA(n)\) in \(v_A\) and \(\pm s_pP(n)A(n)\) in
\(P(D)v_A\), while in \(v_A^2\) it is \(\pm s_p^2S_A(n)\) with the
convolution sum
\[
 S_A(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}A(j)A(n-j).
\]
These sums are the values of a polynomial \(S_A\) of degree \(2p-1\),
and \eqref{eq:main} holds exactly when \(2P(n)A(n)=s_pS_A(n)\) for all
\(n\). So the pair exists if and only if the polynomial \(A\) divides
the polynomial \(S_A\), and then \(P\) is the quotient, up to the
constant factor (Proposition~\ref{prop:divisibility}).

The smallest case shows what happens. For \(p=2\) and \(A=\rho+a\),
\[
 S_A(\rho)=(\rho-1)\Bigl(\frac{\rho(\rho+1)}6+a\rho+a^2\Bigr),
\]
which is divisible by \(\rho+a\) exactly when \(a(a-1)(a+1)=0\). The
solution \(a=0\) is the KdV soliton, and \(a=\pm1\) are the
KdV--Burgers front read in its two directions. Now reduce modulo
\(3=2p-1\). Whatever the value of \(a\),
\[
 3S_A(\rho)\equiv-(\rho^3-\rho)=-\rho(\rho-1)(\rho+1)\pmod3,
\]
so modulo \(3\) the divisor \(A\) must be one of the three factors
\(\rho\), \(\rho-1\), \(\rho+1\). Each of them comes from exactly one
solution, and the root of \(A\) modulo \(3\) serves as a label: \(\{0\}\)
for the soliton, \(\{2\}\) for the front \(a=1\), that is, the root
\(-1\equiv2\), and \(\{1\}\) for the front \(a=-1\).

The same happens whenever \(\ell=2p-1\) is prime. The polynomial
\(S_A\) is a combination of the binomial coefficients
\(\binom{\rho-1}{k}\), and only the last of them, with \(k=\ell\), has
\(\ell\) in its denominator. For this reason \(\ell S_A\) reduces
modulo \(\ell\) to \(\pm(\rho^\ell-\rho)\), whatever \(A\) is, and the
condition becomes that \(A\) divides \(\rho^\ell-\rho\) modulo \(\ell\).
Now \(\rho^\ell-\rho\) is the product of \(\rho-s\) over all residues
\(s\) modulo \(\ell\). Its monic divisors of degree \(p-1\) are
therefore given by the \((p-1)\)-element subsets \(L\) of the integers
modulo \(\ell\), and there are \(\binom{\ell}{p-1}=N_p\) of them. We
prove that every pair can be reduced modulo \(\ell\), although its
coefficients are in general algebraic numbers and not integers, and
that every divisor comes from exactly one pair. An algebraic number is
reduced through an equation that it satisfies: \(i\) is \(2\) or \(3\)
modulo \(5\), because \(2^2\equiv3^2\equiv-1\). For instance
\(A=\rho^2+1/11\), the Kuramoto--Tsuzuki wave, reduces to
\(\rho^2+1=(\rho-2)(\rho-3)\) modulo \(5\), and has the label
\(\{2,3\}\).

So each pair has a label \(L\), and the labels carry information.
The pair is a pulse exactly when \(L\) contains \(0\). At \(p=3\) the
ten classical waves correspond to the ten two-element subsets of
\(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\) (Table~\ref{tab:ks}), and the four pulses are the
subsets that contain \(0\). The mirror image of the pair with label
\(L\) has label \(-L\). The pairs with \(L=-L\) coincide with their
mirror image, and they are exactly the pairs of the two pure
subfamilies. There are \(F_p\) such subsets.

The elliptic count comes from the pulses. When one period of the
lattice tends to infinity, an elliptic pair becomes a pulse, measured
from one of its two constant backgrounds. We show that every pulse, on
either background, arises in this way exactly once, and this gives
\(M_p=2S_p\).

\paragraph{Other orders.}
The number \(N_p\) is also the largest number of isolated solutions,
counted with multiplicity, that matching equations of this shape can
have (Proposition~\ref{prop:bezout}). It is attained if no solution
lies at infinity, which is rigidity, and the solutions are distinct if
all of them are simple. These are the two facts that are not yet proved
at every order. In terms of waves, rigidity says that the rational
wave \(c_\ast z^{-p}\) above is the only one with a single pole
(Lemma~\ref{lem:laplace}). It is proved when \(2p-1\) is a prime or a
prime power, and established by exact computations for \(p\le573\).
Simplicity is proved when \(2p-1\) is prime and, by exact computations,
at \(p=5\), \(8\) and \(11\). Section~\ref{sec:further} states what
follows from each of them; the proofs are in a companion note in the
source package.

\paragraph{Organization.}
Section~\ref{sec:setting} contains the normalizations and the form of
the waves. The counts come first (Sections~\ref{sec:counting}
and~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting}), because they do not use the
classification. Their proofs, in Section~\ref{sec:prime-proof} and in
Section~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting}, can be skipped; every later
section uses only the statements. Then come the classification
(Section~\ref{sec:completeness}), the two pure subfamilies
(Section~\ref{sec:reflection}), a single equation
(Section~\ref{sec:fixed}), the examples (Section~\ref{sec:examples}),
and the other orders with the open questions
(Section~\ref{sec:further}). Appendix~\ref{app:verification} describes
the exact verification and the ancillary material.
