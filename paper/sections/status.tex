\section{Other orders and open questions}\label{sec:further}

The proofs of Sections~\ref{sec:counting}
and~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting} use the prime \(\ell=2p-1\) twice: to
show that solutions cannot escape to infinity, which is rigidity, and
to show that every pair is simple. This section records what is known
when \(2p-1\) is not prime. The proofs use the weighted form of
B\'ezout's theorem (Proposition~\ref{prop:bezout}) instead of the
labels. They are given in the companion note
\texttt{anc/general\_orders.pdf}, which is part of the source package.
The note contains the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:bezout}, the
general-order versions of the results of Sections~\ref{sec:labels},
\ref{sec:elliptic-counting} and~\ref{sec:reflection}, and the proof of
rigidity when \(2p-1\) is a prime power. It also contains the general
lemma on reduction modulo \(\ell\) behind Step~3 of the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}, and a second, algebraic proof of parts~(i)
and~(ii) of Lemma~\ref{lem:conservation}.

\begin{proposition}[Every order]\label{prop:general}
Let \(p\ge2\).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] The isolated solutions of the matching equations,
counted with multiplicity, number at most \(N_p\)
(Proposition~\ref{prop:bezout}), and those with \(\iota A=A\), as
solutions of the matching equations of the pure subfamily, at most
\(F_p\) (Remark~\ref{rem:Fp-bound}).
\item[\textup{(ii)}] If rigidity \((\mathrm R_{p-1})\) holds, all the
systems below have finitely many solutions, and counted with
multiplicity there are exactly \(N_p\) rational-exponential pairs
(Proposition~\ref{prop:bezout}), \(S_p\) solutions of the pulse
equations \eqref{eq:pulse-equations}, \(F_p\) solutions of the matching
equations of the pure subfamily, \(M_p\) elliptic pairs on every
lattice and, for even \(p\), \(2F_p\) elliptic pairs with an even
operator.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] If, in addition, every rational-exponential pair
is simple, then all the conclusions of Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}(a),
Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}, Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric},
Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric} hold at this \(p\), except for
the labelling.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Two hypotheses thus remain when \(2p-1\) is not prime, first at
\(p=5\) (Section~\ref{sec:p5}). This is what is known about them.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Rigidity.} Rigidity \((\mathrm R_{p-1})\) is proved when
\(2p-1\) is a prime or a prime power. Exact integer certificates in the
source package establish it for every \(p\le73\), that is, for every
degree \(d=p-1\le72\), and finite-field Gr\"obner computations, checked
by independent pipelines, for every \(p\le573\) (repository, directory
\texttt{certificates/rigidity\_572/}). Hence the counts with
multiplicity of Proposition~\ref{prop:general}(ii) hold for every
\(2\le p\le573\).
\item \emph{Simplicity.} At the composite indices below \(25\), exact
computations show that all pairs are simple: at \(p=5\) by a Gr\"obner
basis over \(\QQ\), at \(p=8\) over a finite field with Hensel lifting,
and at \(p=11\) by certificates over the \(19\)-adic numbers
(Appendix~\ref{app:verification}). Inside the two pure subfamilies the
computation is much smaller, and it shows that the \(F_p\) pairs are
distinct for every \(p\le14\).
\end{itemize}

\begin{corollary}[Orders up to twelve]\label{cor:twelve}
For every \(2\le p\le12\) there are exactly \(N_p\) rational-exponential
pairs, all simple, of which \(S_p\) are pulses and \(F_p\) belong to
the pure subfamily of that order; and a generic lattice carries exactly
\(M_p\) distinct elliptic pairs. In particular this holds for all the
equations of Table~\ref{tab:named}.
\end{corollary}

At \(p=5\), \(8\) and \(11\) this is Proposition~\ref{prop:general}(iii)
with the two items above; the other orders up to twelve have a prime
index. The first order at which distinctness is not known is \(p=13\).

\paragraph{Open questions.}\label{sec:open}
The first question is rigidity \eqref{eq:rigidity} in every degree;
Lemma~\ref{lem:laplace} says what it means for rational waves. It is
proved in the cases listed above, which include infinite arithmetic
families of higher orders. By Proposition~\ref{prop:general}, it would
establish both enumeration formulas, as counts with multiplicity, at
every order.

The second question is whether every point of \(\Xp\) is simple. The
elliptic simplicity assertion is generic in the lattice and is derived
from this one in Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count}. At a solution
\(S_A=AC_A\), the tangent map of the matching equations is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tangent}
 J_A(h)=\remA\bigl(2(A\star h)-C_Ah\bigr),\qquad \deg h<d=p-1.
\end{equation}
The question is whether this map always has zero kernel. The
prime-index argument proves this after reduction modulo \(\ell\), where
the map becomes multiplication by a polynomial coprime to
\(\overline A\); a uniform replacement is missing at composite indices.
One might expect simplicity to follow from the uniqueness of the
Laurent expansion in Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}. It does not: Laurent
uniqueness concerns a fixed operator, whereas \eqref{eq:tangent} allows
the operator to vary with the profile.

\begin{conjecture}\label{conj:count}
Rigidity \eqref{eq:rigidity} holds in every degree, and the matching
Jacobian \eqref{eq:tangent} is invertible at every point of \(\Xp\).
Consequently there are exactly \(\binom{2p-1}{p-1}\)
rational-exponential pairs, all distinct, for every \(p\ge2\).
\end{conjecture}

The conjecture would also imply \(M_p\) simple elliptic pairs on a
generic fixed lattice at every order, \(F_p\) distinct pairs in the two
pure subfamilies, and that the rational-exponential waves of every
purely dissipative equation are fronts (Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}).
Simplicity on every lattice is false already at order two
\eqref{eq:kdvb-elliptic}; \eqref{eq:ks-collisions} describes the full
exceptional set at order three. Whether a mixed equation of even order
\(p\ge4\) can have meromorphic waves of none of the three kinds is a
separate open question (Remark~\ref{rem:fisher}).
