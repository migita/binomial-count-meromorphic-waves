\section{Other orders}\label{sec:further}

The proofs of Sections~\ref{sec:counting}
and~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting} use the prime \(\ell=2p-1\) twice: to
show that solutions cannot escape to infinity, which is rigidity, and
to show that every pair is simple. This section records what is known
when \(2p-1\) is not prime. The proofs use a weighted form of
B\'ezout's theorem instead of the labels; they are given in the
companion note \texttt{anc/general\_orders.pdf}, which is part of the
source package and contains the general-order versions of
Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}, Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric},
Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric}.

\begin{proposition}[Every order]\label{prop:general}
Let \(p\ge2\).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] The isolated solutions of the matching equations,
counted with multiplicity, number at most \(N_p\), and those with
\(\iota A=A\) at most \(F_p\).
\item[\textup{(ii)}] If rigidity \((\mathrm R_{p-1})\) holds, all the
systems below have finitely many solutions, and counted with
multiplicity there are exactly \(N_p\) rational-exponential pairs,
\(S_p\) solutions of the pulse equations \eqref{eq:pulse-equations},
\(F_p\) solutions of the matching equations of the pure subfamily,
\(M_p\) elliptic pairs on every nonsingular lattice and, for even
\(p\), \(2F_p\) elliptic pairs with an even operator.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] If, in addition, every rational-exponential pair
is simple, then all the conclusions of Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}(a),
Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}, Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric},
Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric} hold at this \(p\), except for
the labelling.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The weights behind (i) are those of Section~\ref{sec:prime}: the
equations \(R_0,\ldots,R_{d-1}\) have weights \(2d+1,\ldots,d+2\) in
unknowns of weights \(1,\ldots,d\), and the quotient of the two products
is \(\binom{2d+1}{d}\).

Two hypotheses thus remain at a general order, and this is what is
known about them.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Rigidity.} \((\mathrm R_d)\) is proved when \(2d+1\) is a
prime or a prime power. Exact integer certificates in the source
package establish it for every \(d\le72\), and finite-field Gr\"obner
computations, checked by independent pipelines, for every \(d\le572\)
(repository, directory \texttt{certificates/rigidity\_572/}). Hence the
counts with multiplicity of Proposition~\ref{prop:general}(ii) hold for
every \(2\le p\le573\).
\item \emph{Simplicity.} At the composite indices below \(25\), exact
computations show that all pairs are simple: at \(p=5\) by a Gr\"obner
basis over \(\QQ\), at \(p=8\) over a finite field with Hensel lifting,
and at \(p=11\) by certificates over the \(19\)-adic numbers
(Appendix~\ref{app:verification}). Inside the two pure subfamilies the
computation is much smaller, and it shows that the \(F_p\) pairs are
distinct for every \(p\le14\).
\end{itemize}

\begin{corollary}[Orders up to twelve]\label{cor:twelve}
For every \(2\le p\le12\) there are exactly \(N_p\) rational-exponential
pairs, all simple, of which \(S_p\) are pulses and \(F_p\) belong to
the pure subfamily of that order; and a generic lattice carries exactly
\(M_p\) distinct elliptic pairs. In particular this holds for all the
equations of Table~\ref{tab:named}.
\end{corollary}

The first order at which distinctness is not known is \(p=13\). We
conjecture that rigidity and simplicity hold in every order
(Conjecture~\ref{conj:count}).
