\section{The count of rational-exponential pairs}\label{sec:counting}

In this section and the next, \(P\) ranges over \emph{all} monic
polynomials of degree \(p\), mixed operators included, and we count the
pairs \((P,v)\) of Section~\ref{sec:setting}.
Nothing in the count requires the hypotheses of
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}. Those hypotheses were needed to show that
the one-pole solutions are all the meromorphic solutions; the matching
equations and their solution counts make sense without them.
The pairs that belong to the purely dispersive and purely dissipative
subfamilies are counted in Section~\ref{sec:reflection}.

Recall the normalization: the rate is one, the pole is at \(i\pi\)
modulo \(2\pi i\ZZ\), and \(v\to0\) as \(e^z\to0\). A wave and its
mirror image are counted separately when they differ.
The functions \(Q,DQ,\ldots,D^{p-1}Q\) span the degree-\(p\)
polynomials in \(Q\) with zero constant term, since
\[
 D^jQ=(-1)^j j!Q^{j+1}+\text{lower powers of }Q.
\]
The leading pole coefficient \eqref{eq:leadingpole} therefore gives
the unique form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized-A}
 v_A=s_pA(D)Q,\qquad
 A(\rho)=\rho^d+\alpha_1\rho^{d-1}+\cdots+\alpha_d,\qquad d=p-1.
\end{equation}
The scale \(s_p\) was defined in \eqref{eq:elliptic-system}.
This representation is the standard singular-part operator form:
\(Q=1/2+D\log\cosh(z/2)\), so \eqref{eq:normalized-A} is a
constant plus a linear differential operator applied to
\(\log\cosh(z/2)\).
Such forms, including the KS case, are discussed by
\citet{ConteMusette2003}.
We use the coefficients \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d\) of that operator
as the coordinates for the enumeration.

The argument runs as follows. The profile \(v_A\) solves
\eqref{eq:main} for some monic \(P\) exactly when \(A\) divides a
polynomial built from \(A\) by a discrete convolution
(Proposition~\ref{prop:divisibility}). The remainder of that division
gives \(d\) polynomial equations in the \(d\) coordinates. When
\(\ell=2p-1\) is prime, these equations become transparent modulo
\(\ell\): they say that \(A\) divides \(\rho^\ell-\rho\). The monic
divisors of degree \(d\) of that polynomial are the products
\(\prod_{s\in S}(\rho-s)\) over the \(d\)-element subsets \(S\) of
\(\FF_\ell\), and there are \(\binom{\ell}{d}\) of them. We show that
every solution has coefficients that can be reduced modulo \(\ell\), so
that it reduces to one of these divisors, and that every divisor lifts
to exactly one solution (Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}). The subset \(S\) is
the label of the pair. Section~\ref{sec:labels} then reads pulses,
fronts and mirror images off the labels, and this gives the count in
the two pure subfamilies (Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}).

\subsection{Discrete convolution and the matching equations}

For polynomials \(F,G\), define \(F\star G\) by polynomial
continuation of
\[
 (F\star G)(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}F(j)G(n-j),\qquad n\ge1.
\]
The continuation exists by the power-sum formulas: for fixed
\(F,G\) the right side is a polynomial in \(n\).
Write
\[
 S_A=A\star A.
\]
For monic \(A\) of degree \(d\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sleading}
 \deg S_A=2d+1,\qquad
 [\rho^{2d+1}]S_A=\beta_d:=\frac{(d!)^2}{(2d+1)!}.
\end{equation}
The leading coefficient is the beta integral
\(\int_0^1t^d(1-t)^d\dd t\).

\begin{proposition}[Polynomial divisibility]\label{prop:divisibility}
The profile \eqref{eq:normalized-A} solves \eqref{eq:main} for some
monic \(P\) if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discrete-divisibility}
 A\mid S_A.
\end{equation}
The operator is unique and is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:operator-A}
 P_A=\frac{s_pS_A}{2A}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the half-plane \(\operatorname{Re}z<0\), the logistic function
is a geometric series in \(e^z\), and applying \(A(D)\) multiplies
the \(n\)th term by \(A(n)\):
\[
 A(D)Q=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n-1}A(n)e^{nz}.
\]
Squaring gives the rational-function identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discrete-square}
 (A(D)Q)^2=-S_A(D)Q.
\end{equation}
The map \(B\mapsto B(D)Q\) is injective: its vanishing would give
\(B(n)=0\) for every positive integer \(n\).
Consequently \eqref{eq:main} is equivalent to
\(2P_AA=s_pS_A\), which has a polynomial solution \(P_A\) exactly
when \(A\) divides \(S_A\).
Equation \eqref{eq:Sleading} and \(s_p=2/\beta_d\) show that \(P_A\)
is monic of degree \(p\).
\end{proof}

Every \(P_A\) has a root among \(1,\ldots,p\). Let \(m\) be the least
positive integer with \(A(m)\ne0\); then \(m\le p\), because \(A\) has
degree \(p-1\). As \(e^z\to0\) the profile begins with
\(s_p(-1)^{m-1}A(m)e^{mz}\) and its square with \(e^{2mz}\), so the
coefficient of \(e^{mz}\) in \eqref{eq:main} gives \(P_A(m)=0\).


The operator determines the profile. This holds for every \(p\),
without the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}, because
it only uses the leading coefficient of a convolution.

\begin{proposition}[One profile per operator]\label{prop:injective}
Let \(A,B\) be monic polynomials of degree \(p-1\) satisfying
\(A\mid S_A\) and \(B\mid S_B\). If \(P_A=P_B\), then \(A=B\).
Consequently a monic operator of degree \(p\) admits at most one
normalized rational-exponential one-pole profile.
Any count of distinct pairs with this normalization is therefore
also a count of distinct operators.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose \(P_A=P_B=P\), and put \(h=A-B\).
If \(h\ne0\), its degree \(e\) is less than \(d=p-1\); write
\(f\ne0\) for its leading coefficient.
By \eqref{eq:operator-A} and the bilinearity and symmetry of the convolution,
\[
 S_A-S_B=h\star(A+B)=\frac{2}{s_p}Ph.
\]
The power-sum calculation in \eqref{eq:Sleading} gives the leading
coefficient \(2f\int_0^1t^e(1-t)^d\dd t\) on the left.
Since \(P\) is monic, the right side has leading coefficient
\((2/s_p)f=\beta_df\). Cancelling \(f\) would therefore give
\[
 2\int_0^1t^e(1-t)^d\dd t
   =\int_0^1t^d(1-t)^d\dd t.
\]
This is impossible: \(e<d\) implies \(t^e>t^d\) for \(0<t<1\), so
the left side exceeds twice the right side. Thus \(h=0\).
\end{proof}

Divide \(S_A\) by the monic polynomial \(A\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:remainder}
 S_A=AC_A+R_A,\qquad
 R_A=\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}R_r(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)\rho^r.
\end{equation}
The matching equations are \(R_0=\cdots=R_{d-1}=0\): \(d\) polynomial
equations in the \(d\) unknowns \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d\).
Their solutions are in bijection with the normalized pairs
\((P_A,v_A)\).
We write \(\Xp\) for the solution set together with the equations
that define it.

Two notions from algebra are used to count. A solution is
\emph{simple} if the Jacobian matrix of \((R_0,\ldots,R_{d-1})\) with
respect to \((\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)\) is invertible there.
Every isolated solution has a \emph{multiplicity}. For a square system
such as this one it may be pictured as the number of simple solutions
into which the point splits under a generic small perturbation of the
equations; formally it is the dimension over \(\CC\) of the local
quotient algebra of the equations at that point. It equals one exactly
for simple solutions.
A \emph{count with multiplicity} is the sum of the multiplicities of
the solutions. It equals the number of distinct pairs exactly when
every solution is simple.

\subsection{The prime-index theorem}\label{sec:prime}

Two tools are needed: a grading of the matching equations, and a lemma
on reduction modulo a prime.

\paragraph{Weights and the continuous convolution.}
Give \(\alpha_j\) weight \(j\) and \(\rho\) weight one, so that \(A\) is
weighted-homogeneous of weight \(d\). This grading reflects how the
coefficients scale: replacing the roots of \(A\) by \(\lambda\) times
themselves multiplies \(\alpha_j\) by \(\lambda^j\). The polynomial
\(S_A\) has weight at most \(2d+1\), and its part of weight exactly
\(2d+1\) is the \emph{continuous} convolution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous}
 H_A(\rho)=\int_0^\rho A(t)A(\rho-t)\dd t
 =\sum_{i,j=0}^{d}\frac{i!j!}{(i+j+1)!}a_ia_j\rho^{i+j+1},
 \qquad A(\rho)=\sum_i a_i\rho^i .
\end{equation}
Indeed these are precisely the highest weighted terms of the power sums
that define \(S_A\). Monic division preserves the highest weighted
part: \(A\) is weighted-homogeneous and monic, so each step of long
division subtracts a multiple of \(A\) of the same weight as the term
being removed. Consequently the matching equation \(R_r\) has weight at
most \(2d+1-r\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weights}
 \text{the part of weight }2d+1-r\text{ of }R_r
 \text{ is the coefficient of }\rho^r\text{ in }\remA H_A .
\end{equation}
In words, the discrete convolution \(S_A\) is a lower-order
perturbation of the continuous convolution \(H_A\), and far from the
origin the matching equations look like the continuous problem
\(A\mid H_A\). Solutions of the matching equations could escape to
infinity only along solutions of that problem. We denote by
\((\mathrm R_d)\) the statement that it has none except the trivial one:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rigidity}
 A\text{ monic},\quad\deg A=d,\quad A\mid H_A
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A(\rho)=\rho^d.
\end{equation}
We call \((\mathrm R_d)\) \emph{rigidity}. The solution
\(A=\rho^d\) corresponds to the rational wave
\(v=c_\ast z^{-p}\) of the operator \(P=D^p\), and rigidity says that
\eqref{eq:main} has no other rational one-pole solution that vanishes
at infinity (Section~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting}, Step~3).

\paragraph{Reduction modulo \(\ell\).}
Let \(\ZZ_{(\ell)}\) be the ring of rational numbers whose denominator
is prime to \(\ell\). For a polynomial \(G\) in
\(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d\), write \(G^{\mathrm{top}}\) for its part of
highest weight.

\begin{lemma}[Reduction modulo \(\ell\)]\label{lem:reduction}
Let \(G_1,\ldots,G_m\in\ZZ_{(\ell)}[\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d]\), and
suppose that the reductions modulo \(\ell\) of
\(G_1^{\mathrm{top}},\ldots,G_m^{\mathrm{top}}\) have no common zero
over \(\overline{\FF}_\ell\) other than the origin. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] \(G_1^{\mathrm{top}},\ldots,G_m^{\mathrm{top}}\)
have no common zero over \(\CC\) other than the origin;
\item[\textup{(b)}] every common zero of \(G_1,\ldots,G_m\) with
coordinates in an algebraic closure \(K\) of \(\QQ_\ell\) is integral:
its coordinates lie in the valuation ring of \(K\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let \(v\) be the valuation of \(K\) with \(v(\ell)=1\), let
\(\mathcal O\) be its valuation ring, and recall that the residue field
of \(\mathcal O\) is \(\overline{\FF}_\ell\) and that, \(K\) being
algebraically closed, every rational number is the valuation of some
element of \(K\).

For (b), let \(\alpha\in K^d\) be a common zero of the \(G_i\), put
\(t=\min_j v(\alpha_j)/j\), and suppose that \(t<0\). Choose
\(\lambda\in K\) with \(v(\lambda)=t\), and put
\(\beta_j=\alpha_j/\lambda^j\). Then every \(\beta_j\) lies in
\(\mathcal O\), and at least one of them is a unit. Decompose
\(G_i=\sum_{w\le W_i}G_i^{(w)}\) into its parts of weight \(w\), so that
\(G_i^{(W_i)}=G_i^{\mathrm{top}}\). A part of weight \(w\) satisfies
\(G_i^{(w)}(\alpha)=\lambda^wG_i^{(w)}(\beta)\). Dividing the equation
\(G_i(\alpha)=0\) by \(\lambda^{W_i}\) gives
\[
 G_i^{\mathrm{top}}(\beta)
 =-\sum_{w<W_i}\lambda^{w-W_i}\,G_i^{(w)}(\beta).
\]
Each term on the right has positive valuation, because
\(v(\lambda^{w-W_i})=(w-W_i)t>0\) and \(G_i^{(w)}(\beta)\in\mathcal O\).
Reducing modulo the maximal ideal of \(\mathcal O\), we find that the
reduction of \(\beta\), which is not the origin, is a common zero of the
reduced polynomials \(G_i^{\mathrm{top}}\). This contradicts the
hypothesis. Hence \(t\ge0\), which is (b).

For (a), suppose that the \(G_i^{\mathrm{top}}\) have a common complex
zero other than the origin. They are weighted-homogeneous with rational
coefficients, so after fixing one nonzero coordinate to be \(1\) the
weak Nullstellensatz gives such a zero \(\alpha\) with coordinates in
\(\overline{\QQ}\subset K\). Define \(t\), \(\lambda\) and \(\beta\) as
before, now for either sign of \(t\). Then
\(G_i^{\mathrm{top}}(\beta)=\lambda^{-W_i}G_i^{\mathrm{top}}(\alpha)=0\),
and the reduction of \(\beta\) is again a forbidden common zero.
\end{proof}

The argument needs the algebraic closure of the residue field; a search
for zeros over \(\FF_\ell\) alone would not suffice.

\begin{theorem}[Prime-index enumeration]\label{thm:prime}
Let \(\ell=2p-1\) be prime, and put \(d=p-1\).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] The matching equations have exactly
\(\binom{\ell}{d}=N_p\) solutions over \(\CC\), and every solution is
simple. Thus there are exactly \(N_p\) rational-exponential pairs, and
they are distinct.
\item[\textup{(b)}] The coefficients of every solution are algebraic
numbers that are integral at \(\ell\). After a choice of an
\(\ell\)-adic embedding of the algebraic numbers, reduction modulo
\(\ell\) is a bijection between the solutions and the \(d\)-element
subsets \(S\subset\FF_\ell\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prime-labels}
 \overline A(\rho)=\prod_{s\in S}(\rho-s).
\end{equation}
\item[\textup{(c)}] Rigidity \((\mathrm R_d)\) holds.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

We call \(S\) the \emph{label} of the pair.

\begin{proof}
We work over \(\ZZ_{(\ell)}\). The proof has four steps: a congruence
for \(S_A\) modulo \(\ell\); the solution of the reduced equations over
\(\FF_\ell\); integrality, which shows that every solution reduces to
one of them; and lifting, which shows that each of them comes from
exactly one solution.

\emph{Step 1: the congruence.}
Induction using \((D-n)Q^n=-nQ^{n+1}\) gives
\[
 Q^n=\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}
        \prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(D-j)Q.
\]
Write \((A(D)Q)^2=\sum_{n=2}^{2p}b_nQ^n\).
Each \(b_n\) is an integer polynomial in the coefficients of \(A\),
and \(b_{2p}=(d!)^2\).
By \eqref{eq:discrete-square},
\[
 S_A(\rho)=
 \sum_{n=2}^{2p}\frac{(-1)^nb_n}{(n-1)!}
       \prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(\rho-j).
\]
Only the last denominator, \((2p-1)!=\ell!\), contains \(\ell\).
Consequently \(\ell S_A\) has coefficients in
\(\ZZ_{(\ell)}[\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d]\), and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prime-collapse}
 \ell S_A(\rho)\equiv
 \frac{(d!)^2}{(\ell-1)!}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}(\rho-j)
 \equiv(-1)^d(\rho^\ell-\rho)\pmod\ell.
\end{equation}
The scalar follows by pairing \(j\) with \(\ell-j\) in
\((\ell-1)!\), and the product is \(\rho^\ell-\rho\) because it runs
over all residues modulo \(\ell\). The right side does not depend on
\(A\): this is what makes the prime index special.

\emph{Step 2: the reduced equations.}
Monic division preserves integrality, so the scaled remainder equations
\(\ell R_r=0\) have coefficients in \(\ZZ_{(\ell)}\), and by
\eqref{eq:prime-collapse} they reduce exactly to
\(\overline A\mid\rho^\ell-\rho\).
Since \(\rho^\ell-\rho=\prod_{s\in\FF_\ell}(\rho-s)\), its monic
divisors of degree \(d\) are the \(\binom{\ell}{d}\) polynomials
\eqref{eq:prime-labels}; all of them are defined over \(\FF_\ell\), and
they exhaust the solutions over its algebraic closure.
Each of them is a simple solution of the reduced equations.
Write \(\rho^\ell-\rho=\overline A C\).
For a variation \(h\) with \(\deg h<d\), the derivative of the
remainder map is, up to the nonzero scalar \((-1)^d\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prime-jacobian}
 h\longmapsto-\operatorname{rem}_{\overline A}(Ch),
\end{equation}
because the dividend \eqref{eq:prime-collapse} does not vary with
\(A\). Since \(\rho^\ell-\rho\) is squarefree, \(\overline A\) and \(C\)
are coprime, so multiplication by \(C\) is invertible modulo
\(\overline A\), and the map \eqref{eq:prime-jacobian} is invertible.

\emph{Step 3: integrality and rigidity.}
We apply Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} to \(G_r=\ell R_r\),
\(0\le r<d\). By \eqref{eq:weights}, \(G_r^{\mathrm{top}}\) is the
coefficient of \(\rho^r\) in \(\remA(\ell H_A)\). Taking the part of
weight \(\ell\) in \eqref{eq:prime-collapse} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prime-continuous}
 \ell H_A(\rho)\equiv(-1)^d\rho^\ell\pmod\ell,
\end{equation}
so the reduced polynomials \(G_r^{\mathrm{top}}\) vanish together
exactly when \(\overline A\mid\rho^\ell\), that is, when
\(\overline A=\rho^d\), which is the origin
\(\alpha_1=\cdots=\alpha_d=0\). The hypothesis of the lemma holds.
Part~(a) of the lemma says that \(\remA H_A=0\) only for \(A=\rho^d\),
which is \((\mathrm R_d)\). Part~(b) says that every solution of the
matching equations with coordinates in \(K\) is integral. Such a
solution therefore has a reduction modulo \(\ell\), and by Step~2 the
reduction is one of the polynomials \eqref{eq:prime-labels}.

\emph{Step 4: lifting.}
Let \(L\subset K\) be a finite extension of \(\QQ_\ell\), with ring of
integers \(\mathcal O_L\). The system \((\ell R_r)_{0\le r<d}\) has
coefficients in \(\ZZ_{(\ell)}\subset\ZZ_\ell\), and by Step~2 its
Jacobian determinant is a unit at each of the \(\binom{\ell}{d}\)
reduced solutions. The multivariable Hensel lemma
\citep{ConradHensel}, whose proof by Newton iteration applies in any
complete discrete valuation ring, then gives exactly one solution in
\(\mathcal O_L^d\) above each reduced solution, and this solution
already lies in \(\ZZ_\ell^d\). Every solution in \(K^d\) has its
coordinates in some such \(L\), and lies in \(\mathcal O_L^d\) by
Step~3. Hence reduction modulo \(\ell\) is a bijection between the
solutions in \(K^d\) and the reduced solutions: there are exactly
\(\binom{\ell}{d}\) of them, and the Jacobian determinant is nonzero at
each.

The matching equations have rational coefficients, and we have shown
that they have finitely many solutions over the algebraically closed
field \(K\). Their solutions over any algebraically closed field of
characteristic zero are then the same finite set of points with
coordinates in \(\overline{\QQ}\). In particular they have exactly
\(\binom{\ell}{d}\) solutions over \(\CC\), all simple. This proves (a)
and (b); part~(c) was proved in Step~3.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Formal verification]\label{rem:lean}
Parts (a) and (c) of Theorem~\ref{thm:prime} have been verified in
Lean~4 against the Mathlib library, both for the matching equations and
for the pairs \((P,v)\) themselves;
Appendix~\ref{app:verification} describes the files and the scope of
the verification. The formal proof follows the route above:
the congruence, integrality of every complex solution, injectivity of
the reduction, and Hensel lifting of every label. For rigidity it
normalizes the roots of \(A\) instead of its coefficients, which avoids
the auxiliary element \(\lambda\).
\end{remark}

The labels \eqref{eq:prime-labels} describe reductions of algebraic
coefficients; they are not assertions that the complex roots of
\(A\), or the characteristic roots of \(P_A\), are integers.
For example, \(A=\rho^2+1/11\) at \(p=3\) reduces to
\((\rho-2)(\rho-3)\) modulo five, so its label is \(\{2,3\}\).

\subsection{Reading the labels: pulses, fronts and mirror images}
\label{sec:labels}

The endpoints of \(v_A\) are its limits as \(z\to\mp\infty\), namely
\(0\) and \(s_pA(0)\). Pairs with \(A(0)=0\) have equal endpoints and
are pulses; the others are fronts. The pulses are needed separately,
because the elliptic pairs of Section~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting}
degenerate onto them.

\begin{proposition}[Pulses and fronts]\label{cor:endpoints}
Let \(\ell=2p-1\) be prime. A pair is a pulse if and only if its label
contains \(0\). Consequently exactly
\[
 S_p:=\binom{2p-2}{p-2}
\]
of the \(N_p\) pairs are pulses, and the remaining
\(\binom{2p-2}{p-1}\) are fronts. Every pulse is a simple solution of
the pulse equations \eqref{eq:pulse-equations} below, and
\(P_A(0)\ne0\) at every pulse. These are counts over \(\CC\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If \(A(0)=0\), then \(\overline A(0)=0\), so \(0\) belongs to the
label. For the converse we show that every label containing \(0\) is
the label of a pulse; since a label determines its pair, this pulse is
the pair with that label.

Polynomial summation gives \(S_A(0)=-A(0)^2\), so the
constant remainder satisfies the polynomial identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constant-remainder}
 R_0=-A(0)\bigl(A(0)+C_A(0)\bigr).
\end{equation}
On the hyperplane \(\alpha_d=A(0)=0\) the equation \(R_0=0\) therefore
holds identically, and the pulses are the solutions of the
\emph{pulse equations}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pulse-equations}
 R_1=\cdots=R_{d-1}=0\qquad\text{in the unknowns }
 \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{d-1},\quad\alpha_d=0 .
\end{equation}
We write \(\mathcal Z_p\) for this system together with its solution
set. Let \(S\) be a label containing
\(0\), and \(\overline A\) the corresponding divisor of
\(\rho^\ell-\rho\). By \eqref{eq:constant-remainder}, at the reduced
solution \(\overline A\) the row of the Jacobian matrix that belongs to
\(R_0\) has a single nonzero entry, \(\pm C(0)\) in the column of
\(\alpha_d\); here \(C=(\rho^\ell-\rho)/\overline A\), and \(C(0)\ne0\)
because \(\rho^\ell-\rho\) is squarefree. The full Jacobian is
invertible by Step~2 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}, so the
minor obtained by deleting that row and that column is invertible as
well. This minor is the Jacobian of the pulse equations. Hensel's lemma
therefore lifts \(\overline A\) to a solution of the pulse equations
over \(\ZZ_\ell\). Completed by \(\alpha_d=0\), it is a solution of the
full matching system above \(\overline A\), hence, by the uniqueness in
Step~4, the solution with label \(S\). That solution is thus a pulse,
and the Jacobian determinant of the pulse equations is a unit there.

The number of \(d\)-element subsets of \(\FF_\ell\) containing \(0\) is
\(\binom{\ell-1}{d-1}=S_p\). Finally \(\ell C_A\) reduces to
\((-1)^dC\), so \(C_A(0)\ne0\) at every pulse, and
\(P_A(0)=s_pC_A(0)/2\ne0\).
\end{proof}
