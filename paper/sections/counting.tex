\section{Enumeration at fixed scale}\label{sec:counting}

In this section \(P\) ranges over \emph{all} monic polynomials of
degree \(p\), including mixed operators of even degree, and we count
the pairs \((P,v)\) in which \(v\) is a one-pole solution of
\eqref{eq:main} of a given kind: rational in an exponential at a fixed
rate here, elliptic on a fixed lattice in
Section~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting}.
Nothing in the enumeration requires \(p\) to be odd or \(P\) to be an
even polynomial. Those hypotheses were needed to show that the
one-pole solutions are all the meromorphic solutions; the matching
equations and their solution counts make sense without them.
The counts are for the full monic family and change for a restricted
family such as the even polynomials \(P\); Section~\ref{sec:kawahara}
gives an example.

For the rational-exponential kind, fix the rate to one, the pole
to \(i\pi\) modulo \(2\pi i\ZZ\), and the value at \(e^z=0\) to
zero. Reflection is not identified: distinct normalized pairs related by
\(z\mapsto-z\) are counted separately.
The functions \(Q,DQ,\ldots,D^{p-1}Q\) span the degree-\(p\)
polynomials in \(Q\) with zero constant term, since
\[
 D^jQ=(-1)^j j!Q^{j+1}+\text{lower powers of }Q.
\]
The leading pole coefficient \eqref{eq:leadingpole} therefore gives
the unique form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalized-A}
 v_A=s_pA(D)Q,\qquad
 A(\rho)=\rho^d+\alpha_1\rho^{d-1}+\cdots+\alpha_d,\qquad d=p-1.
\end{equation}
The scale \(s_p\) was defined in \eqref{eq:elliptic-system}.
This representation is the standard singular-part operator form:
\(Q=1/2+D\log\cosh(z/2)\), so \eqref{eq:normalized-A} is a
constant plus a linear differential operator applied to
\(\log\cosh(z/2)\).
Such forms, including the KS case, are discussed by
\citet{ConteMusette2003}.
We use the coefficients \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d\) of that operator
as the coordinates for the enumeration.

The argument runs as follows. The profile \(v_A\) solves
\eqref{eq:main} for some monic \(P\) exactly when \(A\) divides a
polynomial built from \(A\) by a discrete convolution
(Proposition~\ref{prop:divisibility}). The remainder of that division
gives \(d\) polynomial equations in the \(d\) coordinates. We count
their solutions with multiplicity by B\'ezout's theorem, using a
weighted degree adapted to the equations
(Theorem~\ref{thm:length}). When \(2p-1\) is prime, reduction modulo
\(2p-1\) identifies the solutions one by one
(Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}).

\subsection{Discrete convolution and the matching equations}

For polynomials \(F,G\), define \(F\star G\) by polynomial
continuation of
\[
 (F\star G)(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}F(j)G(n-j),\qquad n\ge1.
\]
The continuation exists by the power-sum formulas: for fixed
\(F,G\) the right side is a polynomial in \(n\).
Write
\[
 S_A=A\star A.
\]
For monic \(A\) of degree \(d\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Sleading}
 \deg S_A=2d+1,\qquad
 [\rho^{2d+1}]S_A=\beta_d:=\frac{(d!)^2}{(2d+1)!}.
\end{equation}
The leading coefficient is the beta integral
\(\int_0^1t^d(1-t)^d\dd t\).

\begin{proposition}[Polynomial divisibility]\label{prop:divisibility}
The profile \eqref{eq:normalized-A} solves \eqref{eq:main} for some
monic \(P\) if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discrete-divisibility}
 A\mid S_A.
\end{equation}
The operator is unique and is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:operator-A}
 P_A=\frac{s_pS_A}{2A}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the half-plane \(\operatorname{Re}z<0\), the logistic function
is a geometric series in \(e^z\), and applying \(A(D)\) multiplies
the \(n\)th term by \(A(n)\):
\[
 A(D)Q=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n-1}A(n)e^{nz}.
\]
Squaring gives the rational-function identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discrete-square}
 (A(D)Q)^2=-S_A(D)Q.
\end{equation}
The map \(B\mapsto B(D)Q\) is injective: its vanishing would give
\(B(n)=0\) for every positive integer \(n\).
Consequently \eqref{eq:main} is equivalent to
\(2P_AA=s_pS_A\), which has a polynomial solution \(P_A\) exactly
when \(A\) divides \(S_A\).
Equation \eqref{eq:Sleading} and \(s_p=2/\beta_d\) show that \(P_A\)
is monic of degree \(p\).
\end{proof}

The operator determines the profile. This holds for every \(p\),
without the parity hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}, because
it only uses the leading coefficient of a convolution.

\begin{proposition}[One profile per operator]\label{prop:injective}
Let \(A,B\) be monic polynomials of degree \(p-1\) satisfying
\(A\mid S_A\) and \(B\mid S_B\). If \(P_A=P_B\), then \(A=B\).
Consequently a monic operator of degree \(p\) admits at most one
normalized rational-exponential one-pole profile.
Any count of distinct pairs with this normalization is therefore
also a count of distinct operators.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose \(P_A=P_B=P\), and put \(h=A-B\).
If \(h\ne0\), its degree \(e\) is less than \(d=p-1\); write
\(f\ne0\) for its leading coefficient.
By \eqref{eq:operator-A} and the bilinearity and symmetry of the convolution,
\[
 S_A-S_B=h\star(A+B)=\frac{2}{s_p}Ph.
\]
The power-sum calculation in \eqref{eq:Sleading} gives the leading
coefficient \(2f\int_0^1t^e(1-t)^d\dd t\) on the left.
Since \(P\) is monic, the right side has leading coefficient
\((2/s_p)f=\beta_df\). Cancelling \(f\) would therefore give
\[
 2\int_0^1t^e(1-t)^d\dd t
   =\int_0^1t^d(1-t)^d\dd t.
\]
This is impossible: \(e<d\) implies \(t^e>t^d\) for \(0<t<1\), so
the left side exceeds twice the right side. Thus \(h=0\).
\end{proof}

For odd \(p\), Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} gives uniqueness among all
nonconstant meromorphic solutions up to translation; for even \(P\),
it does so on a fixed energy level.
Proposition~\ref{prop:injective} fixes the rational-exponential
normalization and holds for every monic operator.

Divide \(S_A\) by the monic polynomial \(A\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:remainder}
 S_A=AC_A+R_A,\qquad
 R_A=\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}R_r(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)\rho^r.
\end{equation}
The matching equations are \(R_0=\cdots=R_{d-1}=0\): \(d\) polynomial
equations in the \(d\) unknowns \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d\).
Their solutions are in bijection with the normalized pairs
\((P_A,v_A)\).
We write \(\Xp\) for the solution set together with the equations
that define it; in algebraic language, \(\Xp\) is the affine scheme
defined by the \(R_r\).

Three notions from algebra are used to count. A solution is
\emph{simple} if the Jacobian matrix of \((R_0,\ldots,R_{d-1})\) with
respect to \((\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d)\) is invertible there;
equivalently, the equations admit no nonzero infinitesimal
deformation of the solution.
Every isolated solution has a \emph{multiplicity}, the dimension over
\(\CC\) of the local quotient
algebra of the equations at that point. For a square system such as
this one it may be pictured as the number of simple solutions into
which the point splits under a generic small perturbation of the
equations; it equals one exactly for simple solutions.
When \(\Xp\) is finite, its \emph{length} is the sum of the
multiplicities of its points, and \(\Xp\) is \emph{reduced} when every
point is simple, so that the length is the number of points.
An isolated solution of multiplicity greater than one is still the
only solution near itself; local uniqueness is therefore weaker than
simplicity.

\subsection{Weighted degree and the role of continuous convolution}

B\'ezout's theorem bounds the sum of the multiplicities of the
isolated solutions of \(d\) polynomial equations in \(d\) unknowns
by the product of their degrees. Equality holds when the system has
no solutions at infinity, that is, when the highest-degree parts of
the equations have no common zero other than the origin. The ordinary
degrees of the matching equations give a poor bound. The right
measure of their size is a weighted degree that reflects how the
\(\alpha_j\) scale.
Give \(\alpha_j\) weight \(j\), and \(\rho\) weight one.
The remainder equations have highest weighted degrees
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weights}
 2d+1-r\quad(0\le r<d),\qquad\text{namely }d+2,\ldots,2d+1.
\end{equation}
Their highest weighted parts are the coefficients of
\(\remA H_A\), where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:continuous}
 H_A(\rho)=\int_0^\rho A(t)A(\rho-t)\dd t.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if \(A(\rho)=\sum a_i\rho^i\), then
\[
 H_A(\rho)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d}
     \frac{i!j!}{(i+j+1)!}a_ia_j\rho^{i+j+1}.
\]
These are precisely the highest weighted terms of the power sums
defining \(S_A\), and monic division preserves the highest weighted
part. Indeed, \(A\) is weighted-homogeneous of weight \(d\), and each
step of long division subtracts a multiple of \(A\) of the same
weight as the term being removed.
In words, the discrete convolution \(S_A\) is a lower-order
perturbation of the continuous convolution \(H_A\), and the behaviour
of the matching equations at infinity is governed by the continuous
problem \(A\mid H_A\).

For this degree, denote by \((\mathrm{R}_d)\) the condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rigidity}
 A\text{ monic},\quad\deg A=d,\quad A\mid H_A
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A(\rho)=\rho^d.
\end{equation}
It says that the continuous problem has only the trivial solution.
By the preceding paragraph, it is exactly the condition that the
matching equations have no solution at weighted infinity.

\begin{theorem}[Count with algebraic multiplicities]\label{thm:length}
For every \(p\ge2\), the sum of multiplicities of the isolated points
of \(\Xp\) is at most
\[
 N_p=\binom{2p-1}{p-1}.
\]
If \((\mathrm R_{p-1})\) holds, then \(\Xp\) is finite and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:length}
 \len\Xp=N_p.
\end{equation}
This equality does not require simplicity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Weighted degree is turned into ordinary degree by the finite
substitution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-cover}
 \alpha_j=y_j^j,\qquad 1\le j\le d.
\end{equation}
A monomial of weight \(w\) in the \(\alpha_j\) becomes a monomial of
ordinary degree \(w\) in the \(y_j\), so the pulled-back equations
have ordinary degrees at most \(d+2,\ldots,2d+1\), and their
highest-degree parts are the pull-backs of the highest weighted parts.
B\'ezout's inequality for isolated solutions bounds the sum of their
multiplicities by
\((d+2)\cdots(2d+1)\); see \citet[Chapter~VIII]{Mondal2021}.
If \((\mathrm R_d)\) holds, the leading homogeneous forms have
no nonzero common zero. The homogenized system has no point at
infinity, and ordinary B\'ezout gives equality
\citep{CoxLittleOShea2005}.

It remains to return from the \(y_j\) to the \(\alpha_j\).
A solution in the \(\alpha_j\) with all \(\alpha_j\ne0\) has
\(1\cdot2\cdots d=d!\) preimages in the \(y_j\), one for each
choice of the roots \(y_j=\alpha_j^{1/j}\), each with the original
multiplicity. In general, for each solution the sum of the
multiplicities of its preimages is \(d!\) times its original
multiplicity, including at branch points where some \(\alpha_j\)
vanish. Thus the substitution multiplies the total length by \(d!\).
Algebraically, \(\CC[y_1,\ldots,y_d]\) is a free module over
\(\CC[\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_d]\), with basis
\[
 y_1^{e_1}\cdots y_d^{e_d},\qquad 0\le e_j<j.
\]
This remains true after imposing the equations and restricting to
an isolated component.
Dividing the B\'ezout count by \(d!\) gives
\[
 \frac{(d+2)\cdots(2d+1)}{d!}=\binom{2d+1}{d}.
\]
The no-infinity condition also gives finiteness of the whole affine
scheme, completing the proof.
\end{proof}

For \(p=3\), the variable weights are \(1,2\) and the equation
weights are \(4,5\), so the calculation is
\((4\cdot5)/(1\cdot2)=10\).

\subsection{The prime-index theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Prime-index enumeration]\label{thm:prime}
If \(\ell=2p-1\) is prime, then
\[
 \#\Xp(\CC)=\len\Xp=\binom{\ell}{p-1},
\]
and every point is simple.
After a choice of an \(\ell\)-adic embedding of the algebraic
coefficients, reduction labels the points by the
\((p-1)\)-element subsets \(S\subset\FF_\ell\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prime-labels}
 \overline A(\rho)=\prod_{s\in S}(\rho-s).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put \(d=p-1\), so \(\ell=2d+1\), and work over
\(\ZZ_{(\ell)}\), the rationals integral at \(\ell\).
The proof has four steps: a congruence for \(S_A\) modulo \(\ell\);
the solution of the reduced equations over \(\FF_\ell\); the
exclusion of solutions at infinity, which proves \((\mathrm R_d)\);
and the lifting of the reduced solutions back to characteristic zero.

\emph{Step 1: the congruence.}
Induction using \((D-n)Q^n=-nQ^{n+1}\) gives
\[
 Q^n=\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}
        \prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(D-j)Q.
\]
Write \((A(D)Q)^2=\sum_{n=2}^{2p}b_nQ^n\).
Each \(b_n\) is an integer polynomial in the coefficients of \(A\),
and \(b_{2p}=(d!)^2\).
By \eqref{eq:discrete-square},
\[
 S_A(\rho)=
 \sum_{n=2}^{2p}\frac{(-1)^nb_n}{(n-1)!}
       \prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(\rho-j).
\]
Only the last denominator, \((2p-1)!=\ell!\), contains \(\ell\).
Consequently \(\ell S_A\) is integral and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prime-collapse}
 \ell S_A(\rho)\equiv
 \frac{(d!)^2}{(\ell-1)!}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}(\rho-j)
 \equiv(-1)^d(\rho^\ell-\rho)\pmod\ell.
\end{equation}
The scalar follows by pairing \(j\) with \(\ell-j\) in
\((\ell-1)!\), and the product is \(\rho^\ell-\rho\) because it runs
over all residues modulo \(\ell\).

\emph{Step 2: the reduced equations.}
The scaled remainder equations \(\ell R_r=0\) therefore reduce
exactly to
\(\overline A\mid\rho^\ell-\rho\).
There are \(\binom{\ell}{d}\) monic divisors of degree \(d\), all
defined over \(\FF_\ell\), and these exhaust the solutions over its
algebraic closure.
Each of them is a simple solution of the reduced equations.
Write \(\rho^\ell-\rho=\overline A C\).
For a variation \(h\) with \(\deg h<d\), the derivative of the
remainder map is, up to the nonzero scalar \((-1)^d\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prime-jacobian}
 h\longmapsto-\operatorname{rem}_{\overline A}(Ch).
\end{equation}
Since \(\rho^\ell-\rho\) is squarefree, \(\overline A\) and \(C\)
are coprime, so multiplication by \(C\) is invertible modulo
\(\overline A\), and the map \eqref{eq:prime-jacobian} is invertible.

\emph{Step 3: no solutions at infinity.}
We must also prove that characteristic-zero solutions cannot escape
to infinity, that is, that \((\mathrm R_d)\) holds.
Taking the highest weighted part of \eqref{eq:prime-collapse} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prime-continuous}
 \ell H_A(\rho)\equiv(-1)^d\rho^\ell\pmod\ell.
\end{equation}
Thus the reduced leading remainder equations have only
\(\overline A=\rho^d\).
After \eqref{eq:weighted-cover}, they are homogeneous equations
with no nonzero common zero over \(\overline{\FF}_\ell\).
The same is true in characteristic zero. If the equations had a
complex projective zero, they would also have one with algebraic
coordinates, since their coefficients are rational.
Normalization at an \(\ell\)-adic place would then produce a nonzero
reduced point.
The valuation argument is stated precisely in
Appendix~\ref{app:verification}.
We have proved \((\mathrm R_d)\), so
Theorem~\ref{thm:length} gives total length \(\binom{\ell}{d}\).

\emph{Step 4: lifting.}
The multivariable simple-root Hensel lemma lifts each of
the \(\binom{\ell}{d}\) reduced points to a unique point over
\(\ZZ_\ell\); see \citet{ConradHensel}.
The lifts are distinct and simple. Their number already equals the
characteristic-zero total length, so they exhaust the scheme.
The equations define a finite algebra over \(\QQ\), hence every
coordinate of a solution is algebraic over \(\QQ\), and the number of
geometric points and their local lengths are the same over
\(\overline{\QQ}_\ell\) and over \(\CC\).
This proves the assertion and the labelling.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Formal verification]\label{rem:lean}
The count and simplicity assertions of Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}, and
\((\mathrm R_{p-1})\) for \(2p-1\) prime, have been verified in
Lean~4 against the Mathlib library; Appendix~\ref{app:verification}
describes the files. The formal proof of the count does not use
Theorem~\ref{thm:length}. It shows directly that every complex
matching point is integral at any valuation ring of \(\CC\) whose
maximal ideal contains \(\ell\): the roots of \(A\) are scaled by a
root of smallest valuation, and \(\ell S_A\) evaluated at that root
has one unit term, all others lying in the maximal ideal. Reduction of
integral points is injective, which gives finiteness and the upper
bound \(\binom{\ell}{d}\), and the same normalization gives
simplicity. For the lower bound, each reduced point is lifted over
\(\ZZ_\ell\) by a multivariable Hensel lemma derived from formal
smoothness, and the resulting family of distinct \(\QQ_\ell\)-points
is transferred to \(\CC\) through the Nullstellensatz, without
identifying \(\QQ_\ell\) with a subfield of \(\CC\). The labelling
\eqref{eq:prime-labels} is the injective reduction map of that proof.
\end{remark}

The labels \eqref{eq:prime-labels} describe reductions of algebraic
coefficients; they are not assertions that the complex roots of
\(A\), or the characteristic roots of \(P_A\), are integers.
For example, \(A=\rho^2+1/11\) at \(p=3\) reduces to
\((\rho-2)(\rho-3)\) modulo five.

The endpoints of \(v_A\) are its limits as \(z\to\mp\infty\), namely
\(0\) and \(s_pA(0)\). Pairs with \(A(0)=0\) have equal endpoints and
are pulses; the others are fronts. The pulses are needed separately,
because the elliptic count of Section~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting}
degenerates onto them.

\begin{proposition}[Equal endpoints]\label{cor:endpoints}
Assume \((\mathrm R_{p-1})\), and let
\(\mathcal Z_p=\Xp\cap\{A(0)=0\}\), with its induced scheme structure.
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pulse-length}
 \len\mathcal Z_p=S_p:=\binom{2p-2}{p-2}.
\end{equation}
If \(\Xp\) is reduced, these are \(S_p\) distinct pairs and
\(P_A(0)\ne0\) at each of them.
In particular, under the prime-index hypothesis, exactly
\[
 \binom{2p-2}{p-2}
\]
normalized pairs have equal zero endpoints. The remaining
\(\binom{2p-2}{p-1}\) have unequal endpoints.
These are counts over \(\CC\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Polynomial summation gives \(S_A(0)=-A(0)^2\), so the
constant remainder satisfies the polynomial identity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constant-remainder}
 R_0=-A(0)\bigl(A(0)+C_A(0)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Set \(\alpha_d=A(0)=0\).
The equation \(R_0=0\) disappears, leaving \(d-1\) equations
of weights \(d+2,\ldots,2d\) in variables of weights
\(1,\ldots,d-1\).
Their leading forms have no nonzero common zero by
\((\mathrm R_d)\). The weighted calculation in
Theorem~\ref{thm:length} gives
\[
 \len\mathcal Z_p
 =\frac{(d+2)\cdots(2d)}{(d-1)!}
 =\binom{2d}{d-1}.
\]
For \(d=1\) the system is empty and its length is one.

If \(\Xp\) is finite and reduced, its coordinate algebra is a product
of copies of \(\CC\); the same is true of any quotient, in particular
that of \(\mathcal Z_p\), so \(\mathcal Z_p\) is reduced as well.
At a point with \(A(0)=C_A(0)=0\), the differential of
\eqref{eq:constant-remainder} vanishes.
Such a point cannot be simple in the square matching system.
Therefore \(C_A(0)\ne0\), and
\(P_A(0)=s_pC_A(0)/2\ne0\), at every point of \(\mathcal Z_p\)
when \(\Xp\) is reduced.
The prime-index assertions now follow from Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}
and \(N_p-S_p=\binom{2p-2}{p-1}\).
\end{proof}

Under the labelling \eqref{eq:prime-labels} of Theorem~\ref{thm:prime},
a pair is a pulse exactly when its label set contains \(0\).
Indeed \(A(0)=0\) forces \(0\) to be a root of the reduced polynomial
\(\overline A\), and the two counts agree: the \((p-1)\)-element subsets
of \(\FF_\ell\) containing \(0\) number \(\binom{2p-2}{p-2}=S_p\), and
those avoiding \(0\) number \(\binom{2p-2}{p-1}=N_p-S_p\).

\subsection{Further orders}\label{sec:further}

The prime-index argument excluded solutions at infinity by reducing
the continuous convolution modulo \(\ell\). The same argument proves
\eqref{eq:length} whenever \(2p-1=\ell^e\) is an odd prime power.
For \(\deg A=(\ell^e-1)/2\), the normalized continuous convolution
satisfies
\[
 \ell^eH_A(\rho)\equiv u\rho^{\ell^e}\pmod\ell,\qquad u\ne0.
\]
Every bilinear coefficient other than the leading one has
denominator valuation at most \(e-1\), whereas the leading
coefficient has valuation \(-e\).
This follows either from the factorial formula or from
\[
 \frac{i!j!}{(i+j+1)!}
   =\sum_{r=0}^{j}\frac{(-1)^r\binom jr}{i+r+1}.
\]
This argument controls only the leading system. It gives the length,
but no simple-root lifting statement for the discrete matching system.

More generally, the exact arithmetic certificates supplied with the
paper establish \((\mathrm R_d)\) for every \(d\le72\), hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coverage}
 \len\Xp=\binom{2p-1}{p-1},\qquad
 \len\mathcal E_p(g_2,g_3)=2\binom{2p-2}{p-2}
 \qquad(2\le p\le73).
\end{equation}
In the same range, Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric} give
\(\len\Xp^{\mathrm{sym}}=F_p\) and, for even \(p\),
\(\len\mathcal E_p^{\mathrm{sym}}(g_2,g_3)=2F_p\).
Together with the ancillary proof, they also cover infinite families
of higher orders.
The certificate covers all complex coefficient vectors in these
degrees. The ancillary proof and the full certificate are described
in Appendix~\ref{app:verification}.
Equation \eqref{eq:coverage} is a statement about lengths only.
At composite indices we establish simplicity here for \(p=5\) only,
where \(2p-1=9\) is the first composite index: a separate exact
calculation proves that all 126 rational-exponential points are
simple; the input and its exact output are supplied in the ancillary
material. Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count} then gives 112 distinct
elliptic pairs on a generic fixed lattice at that order.
Inside the symmetric families a direct exact computation is much
smaller: it shows that \(\Xp^{\mathrm{sym}}\) is reduced for every
\(p\le8\), which includes the composite indices \(9\) and \(15\).

\begin{corollary}[An order-seven consequence]\label{cor:p7}
Among all monic operators of order seven, there are exactly
1716 normalized rational-exponential pairs at unit rate, all simple.
On every fixed nonsingular lattice the elliptic matching scheme
has length 1584, and on a generic such lattice its 1584 points
are distinct and simple.
\end{corollary}

This follows from \(2p-1=13\). As throughout this section, the
operators vary: the corollary counts pairs, not the waves of a single
seventh-order equation.
