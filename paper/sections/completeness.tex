\section{Are these all the meromorphic waves? The classification theorem}
\label{sec:completeness}

The counts did not ask whether an equation can have other meromorphic
travelling waves. For two classes of equations it cannot. The proof is a
local analysis of Painlev\'e type, combined with Eremenko's theorem. A
by-product, Corollary~\ref{cor:symmetric-waves}, identifies the two pure
subfamilies (Section~\ref{sec:reflection}).

\begin{theorem}[Classification]\label{thm:complete}
Assume either that \(p\) is odd or that \(P\) is an even polynomial;
that is, the evolution equation has even order or is purely dispersive.
Every nonconstant meromorphic solution of \eqref{eq:main} is of one of
the three kinds of Section~\ref{sec:systems}: rational in \(z\),
rational in \(e^{\kappa z}\) for some \(\kappa\ne0\), or elliptic. Every
pole has order \(p\), and the solution has one pole per period: its
poles are the translates of one pole by the full group of periods.

For fixed \(P\) of odd degree, there is at most one nonconstant solution
with its pole at zero. For even \(P\), the same statement holds after
fixing the value of the first integral \eqref{eq:energy} below, the
energy. The only entire solutions are \(v=0\) and \(v=-2a_0\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[The hypothesis cannot be dropped: the Fisher family]
\label{rem:fisher}
The travelling waves of nonzero speed of the Fisher equation satisfy
\eqref{eq:main} with \(p=2\) and a mixed operator, the profile equation
of KdV--Burgers. At the special speed of \citet{AblowitzZeppetella1979}
it has a one-parameter family of solutions, given in
\citet{Kudryashov2010}, \citet[Lemma~4.4]{ConteNgWu2015} and
\citet{McCueElHachemSimpson2021}, and for the KdV--Burgers equation
itself in \citet{Kudryashov2009KdVB}; see also
\citet{KudryashovFisher2005}. In our normalization it is
\[
 v(z)=-12\kappa^2e^{2\kappa z}\wp(e^{\kappa z}-1;0,g_3),\qquad
 P(D)=D^2-5\kappa D+6\kappa^2,\qquad\kappa\ne0,
\]
as \((D^2-5\kappa D+6\kappa^2)[t^2U(t)]=\kappa^2t^4U''(t)\),
\(t=e^{\kappa z}\), shows. For \(g_3=0\) it degenerates to the
rational-exponential Ablowitz--Zeppetella front, for \(\kappa=1\) the
front in the first row of \eqref{eq:kdvb-pairs}. For generic \(g_3\ne0\)
it is globally meromorphic and of none of the three kinds; further such
examples are discussed by \citet[Remark~1.11]{NgWu2019}. For mixed
operators of even degree \(p\ge4\) we do not know whether solutions of
other kinds occur.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Earlier work.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} is a uniform version of results that exist in
pieces; for the analytic background see \citet{Eremenko1982}. Odd \(p\)
is contained in \citet[Theorem~1]{YuanLiQi2014}, with related results in
\citet{HuangZhangChenYuan2014,YuanWuChenHuang2014} and a fifth-order
instance in \citet{Dang2023Odd}. For even \(P\), fixing a first integral
to remove the freedom at the resonance is the device of
\citet[Lemmas~3--4]{EremenkoLiaoNg2009} for \(P=D^p+a_0\). It is stated
as a general step by \citet[Section~3.2]{ConteNgWu2018} and
\citet[Section~5]{ConteMusetteNgWu2025}, and applied to the Kawahara
equation \citep{DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010,YeEtAl2022}, the
generalized Bretherton equation
\citep{DeminaKudryashov2011,KudryashovSinelshchikovDemina2011} and the
seventh-order KdV equation \citep{Dang2023Even}; the familiar Kawahara
first integral is the case \(m=2\) of \eqref{eq:energy}.
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} extends the device to every even \(P\),
intermediate derivatives included, with the explicit coefficient
\eqref{eq:energyh}. Equations with two dominant monomials, or without
the finiteness property (finitely many Laurent expansions at a pole),
are treated by other methods in \citet{Demina2019,Demina2022}.

\subsection{The Laurent series at a pole}

At a pole, placed at zero, write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:laurent}
 v(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}c_nz^{n-p},\qquad c_0=c_\ast,\qquad c_n=0\ \ (n<0);
\end{equation}
the pole order and \(c_0\) are those of \eqref{eq:leadingpole}, for
every \(P\). Comparing the coefficients of \(z^{n-2p}\) in
\eqref{eq:main} gives, for \(n\ge1\),
\begin{align}
 I_p(n)\,c_n&=-\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}a_j\prod_{i=1}^{j}(n-2p+i)\,c_{n-p+j}
 -\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_kc_{n-k},\label{eq:recurrence}\\
 I_p(n)&=\prod_{j=p}^{2p-1}(n-j)+c_\ast.\label{eq:indicial}
\end{align}
The product in \(I_p\) comes from \(D^p\) acting on \(z^{n-p}\), and
\(c_\ast\) from the cross term of \(c_nz^{n-p}\) with the leading pole
in \(v^2/2\). The right side of \eqref{eq:recurrence} contains only
\(c_k\) with \(k<n\), so it determines \(c_n\) whenever \(I_p(n)\ne0\).
The positive integer zeros of \(I_p\) are the \emph{resonances} (Fuchs
indices): there \(c_n\) is left undetermined if the right side vanishes,
and no expansion exists otherwise. By \eqref{eq:leadingpole}, \(c_\ast\)
has the sign \((-1)^{p-1}\), and \(|c_\ast|=2p(p+1)\cdots(2p-1)\) is
twice the absolute value of the product at \(n=0\).

Let \(p\) be odd, so that \(c_\ast>0\). The product in
\eqref{eq:indicial} is negative with absolute value less than
\(c_\ast/2\) for \(1\le n<p\), zero for \(p\le n\le2p-1\), and positive
for \(n\ge2p\). Hence \(I_p(n)\ne0\) for every integer \(n\ge1\): there
is no resonance, and exactly one formal Laurent expansion. Theorem~2 of
\citet{Eremenko2006} states that an autonomous polynomial differential
equation with finitely many formal pole expansions and a single monomial
of highest total degree has all its meromorphic solutions among the
three kinds. In \eqref{eq:main} the only monomial of degree two is
\(v^2/2\), so for odd \(p\) both hypotheses hold, for all \(a_j\).

\subsection{The energy argument for a purely dispersive equation}

Let \(p=2m\) and \(P(D)=D^{2m}+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}a_{2j}D^{2j}\). Now
\(c_\ast<0\). The product in \eqref{eq:indicial} is at most
\(|c_\ast|/2\) for \(0\le n<p\), zero for \(p\le n\le2p-1\), and
strictly increasing for \(n\ge2p\), with the value
\((p+1)(p+2)\cdots(2p)=-c_\ast\) at \(n=3p\). So the only resonance is
\(n=3p\), and only \(c_{3p}\) can be free. If it is free, there is a
one-parameter family of formal expansions, and Eremenko's theorem does
not apply to \eqref{eq:main} itself. The remedy is a first integral
whose value determines \(c_{3p}\).

For \(j\ge1\) put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energyterms}
 \mathcal B_j(v)=\sum_{i=0}^{j-2}(-1)^iv^{(i+1)}v^{(2j-1-i)}
 +\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}2\bigl(v^{(j)}\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
where the sum is empty when \(j=1\). Termwise differentiation gives
\(D\mathcal B_j=v'v^{(2j)}\). Since \(P\) contains only even
derivatives, the \emph{energy}
\begin{align}
 \mathcal H_P(v)&=\mathcal B_m(v)+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}a_{2j}\mathcal B_j(v)
 +\frac{a_0}{2}v^2+\frac16v^3\label{eq:energy}\\
\shortintertext{satisfies}
 D\mathcal H_P(v)&=v'\Bigl(P(D)v+\frac12v^2\Bigr),\label{eq:energyidentity}
\end{align}
and is constant on each solution. It is the energy in the usual sense:
an even \(P(D)\) is formally self-adjoint, so \eqref{eq:main} is an
Euler--Lagrange equation, and \(\mathcal H_P\) is the quantity conserved
by its invariance under translation in \(z\).

The resonance is always compatible: if the series \eqref{eq:laurent}
through \(c_{3p-1}\) leaves the residual \(\Omega z^p+\cdots\) in
\eqref{eq:main}, then \(v'\) times it has residue \(-pc_\ast\Omega\); by
\eqref{eq:energyidentity} it is a derivative, which has no residue. So
\(\Omega=0\), and \(c_{3p}\) is free.

But the energy determines \(c_{3p}\): on the formal solution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energyh}
 \mathcal H_P=-p\,c_\ast I_p'(3p)\,c_{3p}
 +(\text{a constant that depends only on }P).
\end{equation}
The terms of \eqref{eq:energy} with \(a_{2j}\) contain fewer derivatives
than \(\mathcal B_m\), so in them \(c_{3p}\) multiplies only positive
powers of \(z\); it enters the constant term only through
\(\mathcal B_m(v)+v^3/6\), in the cross terms with the leading pole.
Linearize \eqref{eq:energyidentity} for \(P=D^p\) about its solution
\(c_\ast z^{-p}\), with perturbation \(z^{n-p}\): if the energy term is
\(\omega(n)z^{n-3p}\), then \((n-3p)\,\omega(n)=-pc_\ast I_p(n)\), and
\(n\to3p\) gives the coefficient in \eqref{eq:energyh}. It is not zero:
the product in \eqref{eq:indicial} equals \(-c_\ast\) at \(n=3p\), so
\(I_p'(3p)=-c_\ast\sum_{j=p+1}^{2p}1/j>0\). Thus the energy fixes
\(c_{3p}\), and \eqref{eq:recurrence} gives the later coefficients.

Eremenko's theorem now applies, not to \eqref{eq:main} but to the level
equation, with \(P\) and the value of \(\mathcal H_P(v)\) fixed. It is
autonomous and polynomial, with the single monomial \(v^3/6\) of highest
degree, and has exactly one formal pole expansion: differentiating it
and dividing by the nonzero series \(v'\) returns \eqref{eq:main}, so
every expansion belongs to the family above, and \eqref{eq:energyh}
selects \(c_{3p}\). Every meromorphic solution of \eqref{eq:main} lies
on one energy level and so is of one of the three kinds.

\subsection{The period group and the entire solutions}

In either case of the theorem, let \(z_1,z_2\) be two poles of one
solution. The functions \(v(z+z_1)\) and \(v(z+z_2)\) satisfy the same
equation and, in the even case, have the same energy. Their Laurent
series at zero coincide, so \(v(z+z_1)=v(z+z_2)\) by the identity
theorem: every difference of poles is a period. Conversely, every period
translates a pole to a pole, so the solution has one pole per period.
The period group is discrete, since \(v\) is not constant. The same
argument proves the uniqueness statements of the theorem: two
nonconstant solutions with the same operator, the same energy in the
even case, and a pole at zero have the same Laurent series there.

An entire solution of one of the three kinds is an entire elliptic
function, hence constant, or a polynomial in \(z\), or a Laurent
polynomial in \(e^{\kappa z}\). The last two are constant as well,
because squaring doubles an extreme nonzero exponent
(Section~\ref{sec:systems}). The constant solutions are \(v=0\) and
\(v=-2a_0\). This proves the theorem.

The same uniqueness shows that, in the two pure subfamilies, every wave
inherits the reflection symmetry of its equation.

\begin{corollary}[Waves are symmetric about their poles]
\label{cor:symmetric-waves}
Let \(v\) be a nonconstant meromorphic solution of \eqref{eq:main} and
let \(z_0\) be one of its poles.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] If \(P\) is even, then
\(v(z_0+\zeta)=v(z_0-\zeta)\).
\item[\textup{(ii)}] If \(p\) is odd and \(P-P(0)\) is odd, then
\(v(z_0+\zeta)+P(0)=-\bigl(v(z_0-\zeta)+P(0)\bigr)\).
\end{enumerate}
Consequently a solution that is rational in an exponential is a pulse in
case~\textup{(i)}. In case~\textup{(ii)} its two limits add up to
\(-2P(0)\), so it is a front unless \(P(0)=0\).
\end{corollary}

The KdV soliton \(3\operatorname{sech}^2(z/2)\) is even about its pole
\(i\pi\). For the Kuramoto--Tsuzuki front, the row \((0,1/11)\) of
Table~\ref{tab:ks}, \(v+P(0)=\tfrac{15}{11}(11\tau^3-9\tau)\) with
\(\tau=\tanh(z/2)\) is odd about \(i\pi\).

\begin{proof}
In case~(i) the function \(v(z_0-\zeta)\) solves the same equation, has
a pole at \(\zeta=0\), and has the same energy as \(v\), because every
term of \eqref{eq:energyterms} and \eqref{eq:energy} contains an even
total number of derivatives. Fixed-energy uniqueness gives~(i). In
case~(ii) the function \(-v(z_0-\zeta)-2P(0)\) solves the same equation,
by Section~\ref{sec:setting}, and has a pole at \(\zeta=0\). For odd
\(p\) the Laurent expansion at a pole is unique, which gives~(ii). The
two limits exist by Section~\ref{sec:systems}, and the statements about
them follow by letting \(\zeta\) tend to the two ends.
\end{proof}
