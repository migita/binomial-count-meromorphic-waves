\section{Are these all the meromorphic waves? The classification theorem}
\label{sec:completeness}

Sections~\ref{sec:counting} and~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting} counted the
one-pole waves that are rational in an exponential or elliptic, for
every operator \(P\). They did not ask whether an equation can have
other meromorphic travelling waves. For two classes of equations it
cannot (Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}). The proof is a local analysis at a
pole of the kind used in the Painlev\'e test, Laurent series and
resonances, combined with Eremenko's theorem. A by-product,
Corollary~\ref{cor:symmetric-waves}, is what identifies the two pure
subfamilies in Section~\ref{sec:reflection}.

\begin{theorem}[Classification]
\label{thm:complete}
Assume either that \(p\) is odd or that \(P\) is an even polynomial;
that is, the evolution equation has even order or is purely dispersive.
Every nonconstant meromorphic solution of \eqref{eq:main} is of one of
the three kinds of Section~\ref{sec:systems}: rational in \(z\),
rational in \(e^{\kappa z}\) for some \(\kappa\ne0\), or elliptic. Every
pole has order \(p\), and the poles are the translates of one pole by
the full group of periods: the solution has one pole per period, in the
sense of Section~\ref{sec:systems}.

For fixed \(P\) of odd degree, there is at most one nonconstant solution
with its pole at zero. For even \(P\), the same statement holds after
fixing the value of the first integral \eqref{eq:energy} below, the
energy. The only entire solutions are \(v=0\) and \(v=-2a_0\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[The hypothesis cannot be dropped: the Fisher family]
\label{rem:fisher}
For even \(p\) the theorem requires a purely dispersive equation, and
this cannot simply be dropped. The known example comes from the Fisher
equation. Its travelling waves of nonzero speed satisfy \eqref{eq:main}
with \(p=2\) and a mixed operator, which is also the profile equation of
KdV--Burgers. At the special speed of \citet{AblowitzZeppetella1979} it
has a one-parameter family of solutions, given in
\citet{Kudryashov2010}, \citet[Lemma~4.4]{ConteNgWu2015} and
\citet{McCueElHachemSimpson2021}, and for the KdV--Burgers equation
itself in \citet{Kudryashov2009KdVB}; see also
\citet{KudryashovFisher2005}. In our normalization it is
\[
 v(z)=-12\kappa^2e^{2\kappa z}\wp(e^{\kappa z}-1;0,g_3),\qquad
 P(D)=D^2-5\kappa D+6\kappa^2,\qquad\kappa\ne0.
\]
Substitution follows from \((D^2-5\kappa D+6\kappa^2)[t^2U(t)]
=\kappa^2t^4U''(t)\), \(t=e^{\kappa z}\). For \(g_3=0\) and \(\kappa=1\)
this is the front in the first row of \eqref{eq:kdvb-pairs}: the
familiar Ablowitz--Zeppetella front is a rational-exponential
degeneration of the family. For generic \(g_3\ne0\) the solution is
globally meromorphic and is of none of the three kinds. Further examples
of meromorphic solutions of none of the three kinds are discussed by
\citet[Remark~1.11]{NgWu2019}. Thus a classification for mixed operators
with \(p\) even must allow a larger class of functions. For mixed
operators of even degree \(p\ge4\) we do not know whether meromorphic
solutions of other kinds occur. Methods for constructing elliptic and
rational-exponential solutions without the finiteness property are
developed by \citet{Demina2022}.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Earlier work.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} is a uniform version of results that exist in
pieces; for the general analytic background, see \citet{Eremenko1982}.
The case of odd \(p\) is contained in \citet[Theorem~1]{YuanLiQi2014},
with related results for odd \(p\) in
\citet{HuangZhangChenYuan2014,YuanWuChenHuang2014} and a fifth-order
instance in \citet{Dang2023Odd}. For even \(P\), fixing the value of a
first integral to remove the freedom at the resonance is the device of
\citet[Lemmas~3--4]{EremenkoLiaoNg2009} for \(P=D^p+a_0\), stated as a
general step by \citet[Section~3.2]{ConteNgWu2018} and
\citet[Section~5]{ConteMusetteNgWu2025} and applied to the Kawahara
equation \citep{DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010,YeEtAl2022}, the
generalized Bretherton equation
\citep{DeminaKudryashov2011,KudryashovSinelshchikovDemina2011} and the
seventh-order KdV equation \citep{Dang2023Even}; the familiar Kawahara
first integral is the case \(m=2\), that is \(p=4\), of
\eqref{eq:energy} below. Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} extends it uniformly
to every even \(P\), intermediate derivatives included. Equations with
two dominant monomials, or without the finiteness property (finitely
many Laurent expansions at a pole), are treated by other methods in
\citet{Demina2019,Demina2022}.

\subsection{The Laurent series at a pole}

We now prove Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}. Write the expansion at a pole
at zero as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:laurent}
 v(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}c_nz^{n-p}.
\end{equation}
The pole order \(p\) and the leading coefficient \(c_0=c_\ast\) were
found in \eqref{eq:leadingpole}, for every \(P\). For the remaining
coefficients, set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:indicial}
 I_p(n)=\prod_{j=p}^{2p-1}(n-j)+c_\ast.
\end{equation}
The product is the factor produced by \(D^p\) acting on \(z^{n-p}\), and
\(c_\ast\) is the factor produced by the cross term of \(c_nz^{n-p}\)
with the leading pole in \(v^2/2\). By \eqref{eq:leadingpole},
\(c_\ast\) has the sign \((-1)^{p-1}\), and
\(|c_\ast|=2p(p+1)\cdots(2p-1)\) is twice the absolute value of the
product at \(n=0\). With \(c_n=0\) for \(n<0\), comparison of the
coefficients of \(z^{n-2p}\) in \eqref{eq:main} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recurrence}
 I_p(n)c_n=
 -\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}a_j\prod_{i=1}^{j}(n-2p+i)\,c_{n-p+j}
 -\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}c_kc_{n-k},\qquad n\ge1.
\end{equation}
The right side contains only coefficients \(c_k\) with \(k<n\), so
\eqref{eq:recurrence} determines \(c_n\) whenever \(I_p(n)\ne0\). The
positive integer zeros of \(I_p\) are the \emph{resonances} (Fuchs
indices) of the recurrence: at a resonance \(n\) the recurrence leaves
\(c_n\) undetermined if its right side vanishes, and has no solution
otherwise.

If \(p\) is odd, then \(c_\ast>0\), and there is no positive integer
resonance. For integers \(1\le n<p\), the product in \eqref{eq:indicial}
is negative with absolute value less than \(c_\ast/2\). For
\(p\le n\le2p-1\) it is zero, and for \(n\ge2p\) it is positive. Hence
\(I_p(n)\ne0\) for every positive integer \(n\), and there is exactly
one formal Laurent expansion at the pole.

Theorem~2 of \citet{Eremenko2006} states that an autonomous polynomial
differential equation with finitely many possible formal pole expansions
and a single monomial of highest total degree has all its meromorphic
solutions among the three kinds. In \eqref{eq:main} the only monomial of
degree two is \(v^2/2\), and for odd \(p\) we have just seen that there
is exactly one formal pole expansion. Both hypotheses hold at every odd
order, for all coefficients \(a_j\).

\subsection{The energy argument for a purely dispersive equation}

Let \(p=2m\) and \(P(D)=D^{2m}+\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}a_{2j}D^{2j}\). Now
\(c_\ast<0\), and there is exactly one positive integer resonance.
Indeed the product in \eqref{eq:indicial} is at most \(|c_\ast|/2\) for
\(0\le n<p\), is zero for \(p\le n\le2p-1\), and is strictly increasing
for \(n\ge2p\); its value at \(3p\) is \((p+1)(p+2)\cdots(2p)=-c_\ast\).
The only positive integer zero of \(I_p\) is therefore \(n=3p\), and the
possible free Laurent coefficient is \(c_{3p}\), the coefficient of
\(z^{2p}\) in \(v\). If the recurrence is compatible at \(n=3p\), the
equation has a one-parameter family of formal expansions at the pole,
and Eremenko's theorem does not apply to \eqref{eq:main} itself. The
remedy is a first integral whose value determines \(c_{3p}\).

For \(j\ge1\), define the quadratic differential expression
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energyterms}
 \mathcal B_j(v)=\sum_{i=0}^{j-2}(-1)^iv^{(i+1)}v^{(2j-1-i)}
 +\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}2\bigl(v^{(j)}\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
where the sum is empty when \(j=1\). Termwise differentiation gives
\(D\mathcal B_j=v'v^{(2j)}\): every even derivative, multiplied by
\(v'\), is an exact derivative. Since \(P\) contains only even
derivatives, it follows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy}
 \mathcal H_P(v)=\mathcal B_m(v)+\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}a_{2j}\mathcal B_j(v)
 +\frac{a_0}{2}v^2+\frac16v^3
\end{equation}
satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energyidentity}
 D\mathcal H_P(v)=v'\Bigl(P(D)v+\frac12v^2\Bigr).
\end{equation}
In particular \(\mathcal H_P\), the energy, is constant on each
solution. It is the energy in the usual sense: an even operator \(P(D)\)
is formally self-adjoint, \eqref{eq:main} is then the Euler--Lagrange
equation of an autonomous Lagrangian, and \(\mathcal H_P\) is the
conserved quantity that belongs to translation in \(z\).

The energy identity has two consequences for the Laurent expansion. The
first is that the resonance is always compatible. Construct
\eqref{eq:laurent} through \(c_{3p-1}\); the first possible residual of
\eqref{eq:main} is \(\Omega z^p\), and multiplication by \(v'\) gives
residue \(-p c_\ast\Omega\). By \eqref{eq:energyidentity} this is the
residue of a derivative of a Laurent series, and is therefore zero.
Hence \(\Omega=0\), and \(c_{3p}\) may be chosen arbitrarily.

The second consequence is that the energy determines \(c_{3p}\). On the
full formal solution the constant energy has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energyh}
 \mathcal H_P=-p\,c_\ast I_p'(3p)\,c_{3p}
 +(\text{a constant that depends only on }P),
\end{equation}
and the coefficient of \(c_{3p}\) is not zero: the product in
\eqref{eq:indicial} equals \(-c_\ast\) at \(n=3p\), so that
\(I_p'(3p)=-c_\ast\sum_{j=p+1}^{2p}1/j>0\). To find the coefficient of
\(c_{3p}\), give \(z\) the weight \(-1\) and \(D\) the weight \(1\), so
that \(v\) has weight \(p\). The highest-weight part of
\eqref{eq:energyidentity} is then the part without the \(a_{2j}\).
Linearize it about \(c_\ast z^{-p}\), with perturbation \(z^{n-p}\). If
the corresponding energy term is \(\omega(n)z^{n-3p}\), then
\((n-3p)\,\omega(n)=-p c_\ast I_p(n)\), and \(n\to3p\) gives the
coefficient in \eqref{eq:energyh}. Lower derivative terms have different
powers of \(z\) and cannot contribute to this coefficient. Fixing the
energy thus fixes \(c_{3p}\), and all subsequent Laurent coefficients
follow from \eqref{eq:recurrence}. This is the resonance-removal step of
the works cited above, with an explicit coefficient for the present
operator family.

We can now apply Eremenko's theorem, not to \eqref{eq:main} but to the
level equation, in which \(P\) and the value of \(\mathcal H_P(v)\) are
fixed. This equation is autonomous and polynomial, and its unique
monomial of highest degree is \(v^3/6\). It has exactly one formal pole
expansion: differentiating it and dividing by the nonzero Laurent series
\(v'\) returns \eqref{eq:main}, so every expansion belongs to the family
above, and \eqref{eq:energyh} selects the value of \(c_{3p}\). Every
meromorphic solution of \eqref{eq:main} lies on one energy level and is
therefore of one of the three kinds.

\subsection{The period group and the entire solutions}

It remains to show that the poles of one solution are the translates of
one pole by its periods. In either case of the theorem, take two poles
\(z_1,z_2\) of the same solution. The functions \(v(z+z_1)\) and
\(v(z+z_2)\) satisfy the same equation and, in the even case, have the
same energy. Their Laurent series at zero coincide, so the identity
theorem gives \(v(z+z_1)=v(z+z_2)\). Every difference of poles is a
period. Conversely, every period translates a pole to a pole. The period
group is discrete: a sequence of nonzero periods tending to zero would
force \(v'\equiv0\). The same argument proves the uniqueness statements
of the theorem: two nonconstant solutions with the same operator, the
same energy in the even case, and a pole at zero have the same Laurent
series there.

Finally, an entire solution of one of the three kinds must be constant.
In the rational case it is a polynomial in \(z\), and in the
rational-exponential case a Laurent polynomial in \(e^{\kappa z}\). In
both cases squaring doubles an extreme nonzero exponent, which \(P(D)v\)
cannot cancel (Section~\ref{sec:systems}). An entire elliptic function
is constant. The constants solve \(a_0v+v^2/2=0\), which gives \(v=0\)
and \(v=-2a_0\). This completes the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}.

The same uniqueness shows that, in the two pure subfamilies, every wave
inherits the reflection symmetry of its equation.

\begin{corollary}[Waves are symmetric about their poles]
\label{cor:symmetric-waves}
Let \(v\) be a nonconstant meromorphic solution of \eqref{eq:main} and
let \(z_0\) be one of its poles.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] If \(P\) is even, then
\(v(z_0+\zeta)=v(z_0-\zeta)\).
\item[\textup{(ii)}] If \(p\) is odd and \(P-P(0)\) is odd, then
\(v(z_0+\zeta)+P(0)=-\bigl(v(z_0-\zeta)+P(0)\bigr)\).
\end{enumerate}
Consequently a solution that is rational in an exponential is a pulse in
case~\textup{(i)}. In case~\textup{(ii)} its two limits add up to
\(-2P(0)\), so it is a front unless \(P(0)=0\).
\end{corollary}

For example, the KdV soliton \(3\operatorname{sech}^2(z/2)\) is even
about its pole \(i\pi\), and the Kuramoto--Tsuzuki front, the row
\((a,b)=(0,1/11)\) of Table~\ref{tab:ks}, has
\(v+P(0)=\tfrac{15}{11}(11\tau^3-9\tau)\) with \(\tau=\tanh(z/2)\),
which is odd about \(i\pi\).

\begin{proof}
In case~(i) the function \(v(z_0-\zeta)\) solves the same equation, has
a pole at \(\zeta=0\), and has the same energy as \(v\), because every
term of \eqref{eq:energyterms} and \eqref{eq:energy} contains an even
total number of derivatives. Fixed-energy uniqueness gives~(i). In
case~(ii) the function \(-v(z_0-\zeta)-2P(0)\) solves the same equation,
by Section~\ref{sec:setting}, and has a pole at \(\zeta=0\). For odd
\(p\) the Laurent expansion at a pole is unique, which gives~(ii). The
two limits exist by Section~\ref{sec:systems}, and the statements about
them follow by letting \(\zeta\) tend to the two ends.
\end{proof}
