\section{The count of elliptic pairs}\label{sec:elliptic-counting}

We now count the elliptic pairs of Section~\ref{sec:setting}. Fix a
lattice \(\Lambda\subset\CC\), including its scale, and put
\(g_i=g_i(\Lambda)\).
We place the pole at zero, allow \(P\) to vary over all monic
polynomials of degree \(p\), and retain the constant term of the
profile. Neither reflection nor a lattice automorphism is
factored out. By Section~\ref{sec:systems}, every profile has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elliptic-enumeration-profile}
 V=-s_p\mathscr D^{p-2}X
       +\sum_{j=0}^{p-3}u_j\mathscr D^jX+b.
\end{equation}
Its only pole on \(\CC/\Lambda\) is zero, of order \(p\).
In particular \(\Lambda\) is its full period lattice.
Let \(\mathcal E_p(g_2,g_3)\) denote the matching equations obtained
by substituting \eqref{eq:elliptic-enumeration-profile} in
\eqref{eq:main}, in the unknowns \(a_0,\ldots,a_{p-1}\),
\(u_0,\ldots,u_{p-3}\) and \(b\), together with their solution set.
The operator coefficients may equivalently be eliminated first, as in
the proof below. Multiplicity and simplicity are understood as
in Section~\ref{sec:counting}.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-lattice elliptic enumeration]\label{thm:elliptic-count}
Let \(2p-1\) be prime.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] On every nonsingular lattice the elliptic pairs are
finite in number, and counted with multiplicity there are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elliptic-length}
 M_p:=2\binom{2p-2}{p-2}=2S_p
\end{equation}
of them.
\item[\textup{(b)}] For \((g_2,g_3)\) in a nonempty Zariski-open subset
of \(\{g_2^3-27g_3^2\ne0\}\), all of them are simple. Thus a generic
lattice admits exactly \(M_p\) distinct elliptic pairs.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The number \(M_p\) is twice the number \(S_p\) of pulses of
Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}, and the proof shows why: when one
period of the lattice becomes infinite, every elliptic pair becomes a
pulse on one of its two constant backgrounds, and every pulse on either
background arises in this way exactly once.
On an arbitrary lattice the assertion is a count
with multiplicities. Exceptional lattices, on which distinct pairs
merge, do occur and cannot be discarded when counting distinct
solutions; Section~\ref{sec:ks} determines them at order three.

The proof has five steps. We eliminate the operator, which leaves
\(p-1\) equations in the \(p-1\) profile unknowns, with coefficients
that are polynomials in \(g_2,g_3\). We assign weights to the unknowns,
under which the highest weighted parts of the equations are obtained by
setting \(g_2=g_3=0\). At this cusp both periods are infinite and the
problem is the rational one, which has only the trivial solution by
rigidity; this means that on no lattice can solutions escape to
infinity. It follows that the pairs form a finite flat family over the
whole \((g_2,g_3)\)-plane, singular cubics included, so that the number
of pairs counted with multiplicity is the same on every fibre. Finally
we count on the nodal cubic, where the elliptic problem becomes the
pulse case of the rational-exponential problem, with two choices of
background; its solutions are simple by
Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}, and simplicity spreads from the nodal
parameter value to nearby nonsingular parameters.

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1: eliminating the operator.}
The coefficient at pole order \(2p\) vanishes by the choice of
\(s_p\).
The coefficients at orders \(2p-1,\ldots,p\) determine
\(a_{p-1},\ldots,a_0\) successively.
At each step the coefficient of the new \(a_j\) is the nonzero
rational leading coefficient of \(D^jV\).
This is a polynomial elimination, valid over any
\(\QQ\)-algebra.
The residual has pole order at most \(p-1\) and zero residue.
It lies in the span of
\[
 \{1\}\ \cup\ \{\mathscr D^jX:0\le j\le p-3\}.
\]
Hence \(p-1\) equations remain in the \(p-1\) profile
coefficients \(u_j,b\).

\emph{Step 2: weights.}
Give \(D,X,g_2,g_3\) weights \(1,2,4,6\), respectively, and give
the profile weight \(p\).
Then \(u_j\) has weight \(p-j-2\), and \(b\) has weight \(p\).
After the triangular elimination, the residual principal-part
coefficients and the residual Laurent constant have weights
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elliptic-weights}
 \begin{array}{c|c}
 \text{variables}&1,2,\ldots,p-2,p\\
 \text{equations}&p+1,p+2,\ldots,2p-2,2p.
 \end{array}
\end{equation}
The Laurent expansions and the eliminations are polynomial in
\(g_2,g_3\), so these statements extend to the singular cubics.
For fixed \(g_2,g_3\), the highest weighted parts of the equations in
the profile variables are obtained by setting \(g_2=g_3=0\), since
\(g_2\) and \(g_3\) carry positive weight.

\emph{Step 3: the cusp.}
At the cusp \(g_2=g_3=0\), \(X=z^{-2}\) and \(Y=-2z^{-3}\), so the
profile is a rational function.
Write \(V=b+f(z)\), where \(f\) has its sole pole at zero
and vanishes at infinity.
The constant and decaying parts of the equation give
\[
 a_0b+\frac12b^2=0,\qquad
 (P+b)(D)f+\frac12f^2=0.
\]
The leading term of \(f\) at infinity forces \(a_0+b=0\).
It follows that \(b=a_0=0\).
Under the polynomial Laplace correspondence
\[
 \sum_k A_k\rho^k\ \longmapsto\
       \sum_k k!A_k z^{-k-1},
\]
differentiation corresponds to multiplication by \(-\rho\),
and multiplication of the rational functions corresponds to
continuous convolution.
After a nonzero scalar normalization, the equation for \(f\)
therefore gives \(A\mid H_A\) for a monic \(A\) of degree \(p-1\).
By rigidity, which holds by Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}(c), \(A=\rho^{p-1}\).
Thus \(f=c_\ast z^{-p}\), and all the variables in
\eqref{eq:elliptic-enumeration-profile} vanish.
The leading equations have no nonzero common zero.

\emph{Step 4: a finite flat family.}
Let \(E_1,\ldots,E_{p-1}\) be the residual equations of Step~1. They
are polynomials in the \(p-1\) profile unknowns and in \(g_2,g_3\),
weighted-homogeneous for the weights of Step~2, and
\[
 \mathcal A=\CC[g_2,g_3][u_0,\ldots,u_{p-3},b]/(E_1,\ldots,E_{p-1})
\]
is the coordinate ring of the family of all the systems
\(\mathcal E_p(g_2,g_3)\). By Step~3 the cusp system has the origin as
its only solution, so \(\mathcal A/(g_2,g_3)\mathcal A\) is a
finite-dimensional vector space. The graded form of Nakayama's lemma
then shows that \(\mathcal A\) is a finitely generated module over
\(\CC[g_2,g_3]\): homogeneous elements whose classes span the cusp ring
generate it. In particular every fibre is finite, which proves the
finiteness in (a) and makes precise the statement that solutions do not
escape to infinity on any lattice.

The family is also flat. It is defined by \(p-1\) equations in \(p+1\)
variables, so each of its components has dimension at least two, and it
is finite over the plane, so its dimension is exactly two. Hence
\(E_1,\ldots,E_{p-1}\) is a regular sequence and \(\mathcal A\) is
Cohen--Macaulay. A Cohen--Macaulay algebra that is finite over a
regular ring of the same dimension is flat over it
\citep[Theorem~23.1]{Matsumura1986}, and a finite flat module over
\(\CC[g_2,g_3]\) is locally free. Consequently the number of solutions
of \(\mathcal E_p(g_2,g_3)\), counted with multiplicity, is the same at
every point of the \((g_2,g_3)\)-plane, the singular cubics included.
At the cusp itself all of this multiplicity sits at the single solution
where every unknown vanishes.

\emph{Step 5: the node.}
To find this common number, and to prove generic simplicity, consider
the nodal parameters
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nodal-parameters}
 g_2=\frac1{12},\qquad g_3=-\frac1{216}.
\end{equation}
In the logistic coordinate they are realized by
\[
 X=Q^2-Q+\frac1{12},\qquad
 Y=(2Q-1)Q(1-Q),\qquad DQ=Q(1-Q).
\]
The nodal coordinate ring is
\[
 \CC[X,Y]=\{f\in\CC[Q]:f(0)=f(1)\}.
\]
Indeed the right side is
\(\CC+Q(1-Q)\CC[Q]\); it is generated by \(X,Y\), since,
with \(S=Q(1-Q)\),
\(S=1/12-X\), \(SQ=(Y+S)/2\), and \(Q^2=Q-S\).
The pole filtration is the ordinary polynomial degree in \(Q\).
Consequently the nodal specialization of
\eqref{eq:elliptic-enumeration-profile} has the unique form
\[
 V=\beta+v_A,\qquad A\text{ monic of degree }p-1,\quad A(0)=0,
\]
where \(\beta\) is the common endpoint.
The coefficient change is invertible and affine over \(\QQ\).

The matching equations become, as systems of equations with their
multiplicities,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nodal-matching}
 A\in\mathcal Z_p,\qquad
 P=P_A-\beta,\qquad
 \beta\bigl(\beta-2P_A(0)\bigr)=0.
\end{equation}
To see this directly, the constant equation is
\(P(0)\beta+\beta^2/2=0\).
The remaining polynomial identity is
\((P+\beta)(D)v_A+v_A^2/2=0\), equivalent by
Proposition~\ref{prop:divisibility} to \(A\mid S_A\) and
\(P+\beta=P_A\).
The coefficient changes and monic division used here work over
arbitrary \(\CC\)-algebras, so the correspondence preserves
local multiplicities.

By Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}, \(\mathcal Z_p\) consists of
\(S_p\) simple points, with \(P_A(0)\ne0\) at every point.
Each quadratic in \eqref{eq:nodal-matching} then has two
distinct roots, and the nodal fibre consists of \(2S_p\) simple points.
By Step~4 every fibre has the same number of points counted with
multiplicity, namely \(2S_p=M_p\). This proves (a).

In the finite coefficient family, the image of the closed locus
where the matching Jacobian vanishes is closed.
Its complement contains \eqref{eq:nodal-parameters}, and hence
meets the nonsingular-lattice locus in a nonempty open set.
Every fibre in that open set consists of simple points, which
proves (b).
\end{proof}

The factor two in \(M_p\) retains the two choices of background in
\eqref{eq:shift}. That transformation preserves the evolution equation
and the lattice; it changes the operator \(P\) and the speed. At the
nodal fibre it exchanges the two roots in
\eqref{eq:nodal-matching}, which are distinct. The two backgrounds are
therefore distinct on a generic lattice, and
the \(M_p\) pairs represent \(M_p/2\) elliptic waves, up to
translations and Galilean shifts, each counted with both of its
backgrounds. On special nonsingular lattices the two backgrounds may
coalesce, as Section~\ref{sec:ks} shows.

\subsection{Elliptic pairs of purely dispersive equations}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:elliptic-symmetric}
Let \(p\) be even, with \(2p-1\) prime. On every nonsingular lattice,
the elliptic pairs whose operator is an even polynomial are those with
\(u_j=0\) for odd \(j\) in \eqref{eq:elliptic-enumeration-profile}, and
counted with multiplicity there are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elliptic-symmetric-length}
 2F_p
\end{equation}
of them. On a generic lattice they are distinct.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
An even operator has an even profile, by
Corollary~\ref{cor:symmetric-waves}. Conversely, if the profile
\eqref{eq:elliptic-enumeration-profile} is even, the odd part of
\eqref{eq:main} is \(\sum_{j\text{ odd}}a_jD^jV=0\); the functions
\(D^jV\) have distinct pole orders, so \(a_j=0\) for odd \(j\).
On the subspace \(u_j=0\), \(j\) odd, the residual of Step~1 is even,
and there remain the equations and unknowns of even weight in
\eqref{eq:elliptic-weights}:
\[
 \begin{array}{c|c}
 \text{variables}&2,4,\ldots,p-2,p\\
 \text{equations}&p+2,p+4,\ldots,2p-2,2p.
 \end{array}
\]
There are \(p/2\) of each. The omitted leading forms vanish identically
on the subspace, so the remaining ones have only the trivial common
zero, by Step~3, and Step~4 applies without change: the even pairs form
a finite flat family over the \((g_2,g_3)\)-plane.
At the node, reflection is \(Q\mapsto1-Q\). An even nodal profile is
\(\beta+v_A\) with \(\iota A=A\), so the nodal fibre is
\eqref{eq:nodal-matching} with \(A\in\Xp^{\mathrm{sym}}\). By
Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric} there are \(F_p\) such \(A\), all pulses,
each a simple solution of the equations of the subfamily, and
\(P_A(0)\ne0\) by Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}. The nodal fibre thus
has \(2F_p\) simple points, and Step~5 applies without change.
\end{proof}

For odd \(p\) there is no fixed-lattice analogue. A purely dissipative
equation has a profile that is \(-P(0)\) plus a function odd about the
pole, and its matching system has one equation more than it has
unknowns. An exact computation on a sample lattice shows that for
\(p=3,5,7\) it has no solution there, and hence none on a generic
lattice. At \(p=3\) such pairs occur exactly when \(g_2=0\), as
\eqref{eq:ks-elliptic-scheme} shows for \(B=0\).
