\section{Mirror images and the two pure subfamilies}\label{sec:reflection}

The numbers \(N_p\) and \(M_p\) refer to the full monic family. We now
count the pairs of the two pure subfamilies. These are the pairs that
coincide with their own mirror image under the reflection
\(z\mapsto-z\). On the labels the reflection is \(L\mapsto-L\), and the
number of labels with \(L=-L\) is again a binomial coefficient.

For a monic polynomial \(A\) of degree \(d=p-1\) put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:iota}
 (\iota A)(\rho)=(-1)^dA(-\rho),\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad
 \alpha_j\longmapsto(-1)^j\alpha_j .
\end{equation}
Proposition~\ref{prop:reflection} shows that \(\iota\) is the reflection
\(z\mapsto-z\) written in the coordinates \(\alpha_j\). It reads the
wave \(v_A\) in the opposite direction and then restores the
normalization: it moves the zero limit back to the end \(e^z\to0\), and
for odd \(p\) it changes the sign of the profile.

\begin{proposition}[Reflection]\label{prop:reflection}
For every monic \(A\) of degree \(d\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reflection-S}
 S_{\iota A}(\rho)=-S_A(-\rho)-2A(0)A(-\rho),\qquad
 R_{\iota A}(\rho)=-R_A(-\rho).
\end{equation}
Consequently \(\iota\) maps solutions of the matching equations to
solutions, and simple solutions to simple solutions.
At a point of \(\Xp\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reflection-pair}
 v_{\iota A}(z)=(-1)^p\bigl(v_A(-z)-s_pA(0)\bigr),\qquad
 P_{\iota A}(\rho)=(-1)^p\bigl(P_A(-\rho)+s_pA(0)\bigr).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

The pairs \(A\) and \(\iota A\) are the mirror images that \(N_p\)
counts separately. By \eqref{eq:reflection-pair} the mirror image
solves the equation in which the terms of one parity have changed sign.
For \(p=3\) the involution is \((a,b)\mapsto(-a,b)\) in
Table~\ref{tab:ks}. For \(p=2\) it exchanges the two fronts \(a=\pm1\)
of \eqref{eq:kdvb-pairs}, \(12Q(2-Q)\) and \(-12Q^2\): indeed
\(12(1-Q)(1+Q)-12=-12Q^2\).

\begin{proof}
Since \(Q(-z)=1-Q(z)\), and \(D\) changes sign under \(z\mapsto-z\),
\[
 \bigl(A(D)Q\bigr)(-z)=A(0)-A(-D)Q=A(0)-(-1)^d(\iota A)(D)Q .
\]
This is the first formula in \eqref{eq:reflection-pair}.
Evaluate \eqref{eq:discrete-square} at \(-z\), and use
\eqref{eq:discrete-square} for \(\iota A\) on the left:
\[
 A(0)^2-2A(0)A(-D)Q-S_{\iota A}(D)Q=-S_A(0)+S_A(-D)Q .
\]
The constant terms cancel because \(S_A(0)=-A(0)^2\)
(Section~\ref{sec:labels}). The remaining terms, and the injectivity of
\(F\mapsto F(D)Q\), give the first identity in
\eqref{eq:reflection-S}. Now substitute \(S_A=AC_A+R_A\) at \(-\rho\):
\[
 S_{\iota A}(\rho)
 =(\iota A)(\rho)\,(-1)^p\bigl(C_A(-\rho)+2A(0)\bigr)-R_A(-\rho).
\]
Division by the monic polynomial \(\iota A\) is unique. Hence
\(R_{\iota A}(\rho)=-R_A(-\rho)\) and
\(C_{\iota A}(\rho)=(-1)^p\bigl(C_A(-\rho)+2A(0)\bigr)\).
The first relation reads \(R_r(\iota\alpha)=(-1)^{r+1}R_r(\alpha)\), so
the invertible linear map \eqref{eq:iota} takes each matching equation
to plus or minus a matching equation. It therefore maps solutions to
solutions, and simple solutions to simple solutions. The second relation
and \eqref{eq:operator-A} give the formula for \(P_{\iota A}\).
\end{proof}

A pair is fixed by \(\iota\) when \(\alpha_j=0\) for every odd \(j\).
We write \(\Xp^{\mathrm{sym}}\) for the set of these pairs, and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fp}
 F_p=\binom{p-1}{\lfloor (p-1)/2\rfloor}.
\end{equation}
Its first values are \(1,2,3,6,10,20\) for \(p=2,\ldots,7\);
Table~\ref{tab:named} lists the equations to which they belong.
On the subspace \(\{\alpha_j=0,\ j\text{ odd}\}\) we have
\(\iota A=A\), and \eqref{eq:reflection-S} shows that \(R_A\) is an odd
polynomial. The equations \(R_r=0\) with \(r\) even therefore hold
identically there, and the \emph{matching equations of the subfamily}
are the equations \(R_r=0\), \(r\) odd, in the unknowns \(\alpha_j\),
\(j\) even. Their solutions are the pairs of \(\Xp^{\mathrm{sym}}\).

\begin{theorem}[Count in the two pure subfamilies]\label{thm:symmetric}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] For every \(p\), a pair \(A\) coincides with its
mirror image, \(\iota A=A\), if and only if \(P_A\) is
reflection-symmetric. Thus the pairs that coincide with their mirror
image, the pairs of \(\Xp^{\mathrm{sym}}\), are exactly the
rational-exponential pairs of the purely dispersive equations when
\(p\) is even, and of the purely dissipative equations when \(p\) is
odd.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] Let \(\ell=2p-1\) be prime. If \(A\) has label
\(L\), then \(\iota A\) has label \(-L\). Hence \(\iota A=A\) if and
only if \(L=-L\), and there are exactly \(F_p\) such pairs. Each of
them is a simple solution of the matching equations of its subfamily.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] The rational-exponential pairs of a purely
dispersive equation are pulses, for every even \(p\). Those of a purely
dissipative equation are fronts: when \(2p-1\) is prime, and for pairs
with real coefficients at every odd \(p\).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

In Table~\ref{tab:ks} the two pairs with \(a=0\) are the \(F_3=2\) pairs
of the purely dissipative subfamily, the Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation
without dispersion. Their labels \(\{1,4\}\) and \(\{2,3\}\) satisfy
\(L=-L\). Both are fronts; the pulses \((\pm1,0)\) need dispersion.
For even \(p\) see the Kawahara family (Section~\ref{sec:kawahara}).

\begin{remark}[Every order]\label{rem:Fp-bound}
At every order the isolated solutions with \(\iota A=A\) of the
matching equations of the subfamily, counted with multiplicity, number
at most \(F_p\) (companion note, Theorem~4.1; the weights of the
restricted system give the B\'ezout number \(F_p\)).
Section~\ref{sec:further} records what is known when \(2p-1\) is not
prime.
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}]
\emph{(i).} If \(\iota A=A\), then \eqref{eq:reflection-pair} gives
\(P_A(\rho)=(-1)^p(P_A(-\rho)+s_pA(0))\). For even \(p\) the
polynomial \(A\) is odd, so \(A(0)=0\) and \(P_A\) is even. For odd
\(p\) the sum \(P_A(\rho)+P_A(-\rho)\) is the constant \(-s_pA(0)\).
In both cases \(P_A\) is reflection-symmetric, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:symmetric-endpoint}
 s_pA(0)=-2P_A(0)\qquad(p\text{ odd}).
\end{equation}

Conversely, let \(P=P_A\) be reflection-symmetric, and apply
Corollary~\ref{cor:symmetric-waves} at the pole \(i\pi\), using the
period \(2\pi i\). If \(p\) is even, then \(v_A(-z)=v_A(z)\). Letting
\(z\to-\infty\) gives \(A(0)=0\), and \eqref{eq:reflection-pair}
becomes \(v_{\iota A}=v_A\). If \(p\) is odd, then
\(-v_A(-z)=v_A(z)+2P(0)\), and \eqref{eq:reflection-pair} becomes
\(v_{\iota A}(z)=v_A(z)+2P(0)+s_pA(0)\). Letting \(z\to-\infty\) shows
that the constant vanishes. In both cases \(v_{\iota A}=v_A\), hence
\(\iota A=A\) by the uniqueness of the form \eqref{eq:normalized-A}.

\emph{(ii).} The map \eqref{eq:iota} negates the roots of
\(\overline A\), so the label of \(\iota A\) is \(-L\). A label
determines its pair (Theorem~\ref{thm:prime}), so \(\iota A=A\) exactly
when \(L=-L\). To count these labels, note that the nonzero residues
modulo \(\ell=2d+1\) form \(d\) pairs \(\{s,-s\}\). A set \(L=-L\) of
\(d\) elements consists of \(d/2\) such pairs if \(d\) is even, and of
\(0\) and \((d-1)/2\) pairs if \(d\) is odd. In both cases there are
\(\binom{d}{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}=F_p\) sets.

Differentiating the relation
\(R_r(\iota\alpha)=(-1)^{r+1}R_r(\alpha)\) at a fixed point shows that
\(\partial R_r/\partial\alpha_j=0\) unless \(j\equiv r+1\) modulo two.
The Jacobian matrix of the full system therefore splits into two square
blocks. It is invertible, so both blocks are, and one of them is the
Jacobian of the equations of the subfamily.

\emph{(iii).} For even \(p\) the polynomial \(A\) is odd, so \(A(0)=0\).
Let \(p\) be odd. If \(2p-1\) is prime, a label with \(L=-L\) has an
even number \(d\) of elements, so it does not contain \(0\), and the
pair is a front by Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}. For a general odd
\(p\), suppose that a point of \(\Xp^{\mathrm{sym}}\) with real
coefficients is a pulse, \(A(0)=0\). Then \(P_A(0)=0\) by
\eqref{eq:symmetric-endpoint}. Now \(v_A\) is real on the real axis,
and it tends to zero with all its derivatives at both ends. Integrating
\eqref{eq:main} along the real axis, and using \(P_A(0)=0\), gives
\(\int v_A^2\dd z=0\). Hence \(v_A=0\), which is impossible.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Elliptic pairs of purely dispersive equations.}
Elliptic waves occur in both subfamilies; the distinction between pulses
and fronts does not apply to them. For even \(p\) the count of
Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count} restricts to the subfamily.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:elliptic-symmetric}
Let \(p\) be even, with \(2p-1\) prime. On every lattice,
the elliptic pairs whose operator is an even polynomial are those with
\(u_j=0\) for odd \(j\) in \eqref{eq:elliptic-enumeration-profile}, and
counted with multiplicity there are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:elliptic-symmetric-length}
 2F_p
\end{equation}
of them. On a generic lattice they are distinct.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
An even operator has an even profile, by
Corollary~\ref{cor:symmetric-waves}, and the profile
\eqref{eq:elliptic-enumeration-profile} is even exactly when \(u_j=0\)
for odd \(j\). Conversely, if the profile is even, the odd part of
\eqref{eq:main} is \(\sum_{j\text{ odd}}a_jD^jV=0\); the functions
\(D^jV\) have distinct pole orders, so \(a_j=0\) for odd \(j\).
We follow the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count} on the subspace
\(u_j=0\), \(j\) odd. There the residual of Step~1 is even, and there
remain the unknowns and the equations of even weight in the table
\eqref{eq:elliptic-weights} of Step~2: the weights
\(2,4,\ldots,p-2,p\) and \(p+2,p+4,\ldots,2p-2,2p\), with \(p/2\) of
each. The omitted leading forms vanish identically on the subspace, so
the remaining ones have only the trivial common zero, by Step~3.
Lemma~\ref{lem:conservation} therefore applies to the restricted system,
and multiplicities refer to this system. For the nodal parameters
\eqref{eq:nodal-parameters}, reflection is \(Q\mapsto1-Q\). An even
nodal profile is \(\beta+v_A\) with \(\iota A=A\), so the computation of
Step~5 gives the system \eqref{eq:nodal-matching} with
\(A\in\Xp^{\mathrm{sym}}\): the matching equations of the subfamily, and
the quadratic for \(\beta\). By Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric} there are
\(F_p\) such \(A\), all pulses, each a simple solution of the equations
of the subfamily, and \(P_A(0)\ne0\) by
Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints}. For the nodal parameters the restricted
system thus has \(2F_p\) solutions, all simple, and the rest of Step~5
applies to it without change.
\end{proof}

For odd \(p\) there is no fixed-lattice analogue. A purely dissipative
equation has a profile that is \(-P(0)\) plus a function odd about the
pole (Corollary~\ref{cor:symmetric-waves}), and its matching system has
one equation more than it has unknowns. An exact computation on a sample
lattice (the script \texttt{elliptic\_dissipative.py} of the ancillary
files, Appendix~\ref{app:verification}) shows that for \(p=3,5,7\) it
has no solution there, and hence none on a generic lattice: rigidity
holds at these orders (Section~\ref{sec:further}), so
Lemma~\ref{lem:conservation} applies as in Step~4 of the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count}, and the elimination in the proof of
part~(iii) of the lemma shows that the parameters at which such
pairs exist form an algebraic subset of the \((g_2,g_3)\)-plane,
invariant under rescaling as in the proof of part~(b) of that theorem.
These are the two entries \(0\) of
Table~\ref{tab:named}. At \(p=3\) such pairs occur exactly when
\(g_2=0\): a purely dissipative operator is \(P=D^3+\mu D+a_0\), that
is, \(B=0\) in \eqref{eq:ks-elliptic-scheme}, which then forces
\(\mu=0\) and \(g_2=0\). At \(p=5\) elliptic waves of the Nikolaevskiy
equation on special lattices are given by \citet{Kudryashov2004} and
\citet{KudryashovDemina2007}.
