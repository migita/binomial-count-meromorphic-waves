\section{Three classical equations}\label{sec:examples}

The three examples make both counts concrete and connect them to the
classical literature. The first two use the full operator families of
orders two and three, so their counts are the values \(N_2,M_2\) and
\(N_3,M_3\) of the general formulas. The Kawahara equation restricts
the order-four operator to an even polynomial; its counts are the
symmetric values \(F_4\) and \(2F_4\) of Section~\ref{sec:reflection},
not \(N_4\) and \(M_4\).
Table~\ref{tab:example-counts} summarizes the results.
The normalizations are those of Section~\ref{sec:counting}: for
rational-exponential profiles the rate is one and the left endpoint is
zero, reflection is retained, and for elliptic pairs both choices of
constant equilibrium are retained.

\subsection{KdV--Burgers and its KdV limit}\label{sec:kdvb}

The integrated travelling-wave equation for
\[
 u_t+uu_x+\nu u_{xx}+\delta u_{xxx}=0,\qquad \delta\ne0,
\]
has order \(p=2\).
Its exact front solutions have been studied in several equivalent
forms \citep{JeffreyXu1989,VliegHalford1991,Kudryashov2009Errors}.
The long-wave reductions in
\citet{KudryashovSinelshchikovChernyavsky2008}
also contain this balance of dissipation and dispersion.

Here \(A(\rho)=\rho+a\) has a single coefficient, and the matching
system is one equation in one unknown. Direct polynomial division
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kdvb-matching}
 \operatorname{rem}_A S_A=-\frac{a(a-1)(a+1)}6,
 \qquad
 P_A=\rho^2+5a\rho+a^2-6a-1,
 \qquad
 v_A=12\bigl((a+1)Q-Q^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
Thus there are exactly three simple pairs:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kdvb-pairs}
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 a&P_A(\rho)&v_A\\ \hline
 -1&(\rho-2)(\rho-3)&-12Q^2\\
 0&\rho^2-1&12Q(1-Q)=3\operatorname{sech}^2(z/2)\\
 1&(\rho-1)(\rho+6)&12Q(2-Q).
 \end{array}
\end{equation}
The two fronts have nonzero Burgers coefficient, while the pulse
belongs to the KdV limit \(\nu=0\).
To return to the original equation with a general rate
\(\kappa\ne0\), set
\[
 u(x,t)=u_\ast+\delta\kappa^2
            v_A\bigl(\kappa(x-ct)\bigr),\qquad
 \nu=\delta\kappa(5a),\qquad
 u_\ast-c=\delta\kappa^2(a^2-6a-1).
\]
Differentiating the normalized equation verifies the original PDE.
In particular, a nonzero \(\nu\) fixes the front rate to
\(\kappa=\nu/(5a\delta)\), \(a=\pm1\), in agreement with the
classical parameter restriction.

For a fixed lattice the elliptic candidate is \(V=-12X+b\).
Its residual is
\[
 -12a_1Y-12(a_0+b)X+6g_2+a_0b+\frac12b^2.
\]
Consequently the complete elliptic system is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kdvb-elliptic}
 a_1=0,\qquad
 v=-12\wp-a_0,\qquad a_0^2=12g_2.
\end{equation}
There is no one-pole elliptic member with nonzero Burgers coefficient.
For \(g_2\ne0\) the fixed lattice gives two simple pairs.
For \(g_2=0\), \(g_3\ne0\), it gives one pair of multiplicity two.
This is \(M_2=2\), including its exceptional specialization.

At the fixed operator \(P=D^2-1\), equation
\eqref{eq:kdvb-elliptic} fixes \(g_2=1/12\) and leaves \(g_3\)
free, subject to nonsingularity.
These solutions lie on different energy levels, as
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} requires.
At \(g_3=0\), a translate by the imaginary half-period is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kdv-cnoidal}
 v(z)=1+\sqrt3\,\operatorname{cn}^2
       \left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{2\sqrt3}}\,\middle|\,\frac12\right).
\end{equation}
The second argument is the Jacobi parameter
\citep[Chapters~22--23]{DLMF}.
In the nodal limit \(g_3=-1/216\), the bounded translate becomes
\(3\operatorname{sech}^2(z/2)\), the middle row of
\eqref{eq:kdvb-pairs}.
The cusp \(g_2=g_3=0\) gives \(P=D^2\), \(v=-12z^{-2}\).

Since \(2p-1=3\) is prime, the one-pole counts at order two are
unconditional.
Global meromorphic completeness from Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}
applies to the KdV subfamily \(a_1=0\).
For mixed \(P\), the Fisher example in Section~\ref{sec:open}
explains why the same global conclusion cannot be imposed.

\subsection{Kuramoto--Sivashinsky: ten pairs and lattice collisions}
\label{sec:ks}

Let \(p=3\), \(s_3=60\), and \(A(\rho)=\rho^2+a\rho+b\).
The profile and monic operator are
\begin{align}
 \frac{v_A}{60}
   &=2Q^3-(a+3)Q^2+(a+b+1)Q, \label{eq:ksprofile}\\
 P_A(\rho)
   &=\rho^3+4a\rho^2+(a^2+19b)\rho
            -a^3+7ab-5a-30b. \label{eq:ksoperator}
\end{align}
The remainder of \(S_A\) is \((E_1\rho+E_0)/30\), where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ksmatching}
 E_0=ab(a^2-7b+5),\qquad
 E_1=a^4-8a^2b+11b^2+10b-1.
\end{equation}
The system \(E_0=E_1=0\) is solved by cases on the factors of
\(E_0\).
If \(b=0\), then \(a^4=1\).
If \(a=0\), then \((b+1)(11b-1)=0\).
In the remaining case \(a^2=7b-5\), and the second equation becomes
\(4(b-2)(b-3)=0\).
This exhausts the system and gives Table~\ref{tab:ks}.
The Jacobian determinant is nonzero at every point, so all ten
pairs are simple, as Theorem~\ref{thm:prime} predicts for
\(2p-1=5\).

There are ten complex pairs, eight with real coefficients.
All eight have smooth bounded representatives on the real axis:
two pulses and six fronts.
For example,
\[
 A=(\rho-1)(\rho-2),\qquad
 v_A=120Q^3,\qquad
 P_A=(\rho-3)(\rho-4)(\rho-5).
\]
The choice \(A=\rho(\rho+1)\) gives the positive pulse
\(v_A=120Q(1-Q)^2\).
These belong to the classical hyperbolic-function solution sector
\citep{KuramotoTsuzuki1976,Kudryashov1988,Kudryashov1990}.
The complete parameter list is tabulated by
\citet[Table~1]{KudryashovZargaryan1996} and
\citet[Table~1]{ConteMusette2009}.
Their tables contain six parameter cases because they identify the
two signs of the dispersion coefficient: two cases have zero
dispersion and four have nonzero dispersion, and restoring the sign of
the latter gives the ten pairs of Table~\ref{tab:ks}.
In the language of Section~\ref{sec:reflection}, the six cases are the
orbits of the reflection \((a,b)\mapsto(-a,b)\), and the two cases
with \(a=0\) are the \(F_3=2\) symmetric pairs. By
Corollary~\ref{cor:solvable}, an equation with
\(P=D^3+BD^2+\mu D+a_0\) has such a wave exactly when
\(B^2=\sigma\mu\) for one of the four values \eqref{eq:ks-sigma}.
The pairs \((\pm1,0)\) and \((\pm i,0)\) belong to the same
equations, with \(\sigma=16\); their rates differ by a factor \(i\), so
for a real equation the second wave is periodic in \(x\), and its real
form has poles on the real axis.
Exhaustion of this classical list follows from
\citet{Eremenko2006}.

For comparison with the usual integrated KS equation, write
\[
 P=D^3+B D^2+\mu D+a_0,\qquad w=v+a_0.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:classicalKS}
 w'''+B w''+\mu w'+\frac12w^2+K=0,\qquad
 K=-\frac{a_0^2}{2}.
\end{equation}
An elliptic solution must have zero residue at its pole.
The Laurent residue is \(60\mu/19-15B^2/76\), and
its vanishing gives \(B^2=16\mu\).
The resulting elliptic formula is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kselliptic}
 w=-60\wp'-15B\wp-\frac{B^3}{64},\qquad
 g_2=\frac{B^4}{3072},\qquad
 g_3=\frac{K+13B^6/4096}{1080}.
\end{equation}
This is the classical solution of \citet{Kudryashov1990};
necessity of its elliptic parameter restriction is proved by
\citet{Hone2005}, and the classification of all meromorphic KS
solutions is completed by \citet{Eremenko2006}.
With leading coefficient \(\nu\), the residue condition is
\(B^2=16\nu\mu\).

The classical formula gives the lattice in terms of the operator.
For enumeration we read it the other way: fix \(g_2,g_3\) and solve
for \(B,a_0\).
The entire elliptic matching scheme is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ks-elliptic-scheme}
 \begin{split}
 P&=D^3+B D^2+\frac{B^2}{16}D+a_0,\\
 v&=-60\wp'-15B\wp-\frac{B^3}{64}-a_0,\\
 B^4&=3072g_2,\qquad
 a_0^2=\frac{13B^6}{2048}-2160g_3.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
The coefficients eliminated along the way are determined linearly
with nonzero rational pivots, so the last two equations carry the
full matching system, multiplicities included.
Their quotient algebra has basis \(B^i a_0^j\),
\(0\le i<4\), \(0\le j<2\), and length eight, which is \(M_3\).

Generically these are eight distinct pairs; the exceptional cases
are found as follows. Let
\[
 j(\Lambda)=\frac{1728g_2^3}{g_2^3-27g_3^2}.
\]
The Jacobian of the last two equations in
\eqref{eq:ks-elliptic-scheme} is a nonzero constant times \(B^3a_0\).
If \(B=0\), then \(g_2=0\), hence \(j=0\).
If \(a_0=0\) and the lattice is nonsingular, elimination gives
\[
 675g_3^2=169g_2^3,\qquad j=-300.
\]
These conditions are sufficient as well. Thus:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ks-collisions}
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 j(\Lambda)&\text{distinct pairs}&\text{local multiplicities}\\ \hline
 j\notin\{0,-300\}&8&1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1\\
 -300&6&2,2,1,1,1,1\\
 0&2&4,4.
 \end{array}
\end{equation}
For \(j=-300\), all four \(B\)-roots are simple, and precisely
two give \(a_0=0\), producing the two double points.
For \(j=0\), the equations reduce to
\(B^4=0\), \(a_0^2=-2160g_3\), producing two points of length four.
For instance, \(g_2=1/12\), \(g_3=13/1080\) gives the double
points \((B,a_0)=(4,0),(-4,0)\).
This calculation exhibits explicitly the exceptional set in
Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count}.

The exponential and elliptic counts refer to varying operators.
No operator in Table~\ref{tab:ks} also has a nonsingular elliptic
solution: the six unequal-endpoint pairs violate the residue
condition, while the four equal-endpoint pairs give singular
invariants in \eqref{eq:kselliptic}.
This agrees with uniqueness up to translation for fixed \(P\).
For \(P=D^3\), the rational member is \(v=120z^{-3}\).

There is also a direct connection with the vessel model of
\citet[Eq.~(4.12)]{KudryashovSinelshchikovChernyavsky2008}.
Its quadratic long-wave equation has coefficients
\(-\lambda/(2\alpha)\), \((\gamma-\beta)/(2\alpha)\) and
\(\delta/(2\alpha)\) at the second, third and fourth spatial
derivatives. When its quadratic advection coefficient and
\(\alpha\delta\) are nonzero, amplitude normalization puts it
in the same travelling-wave class.
The elliptic condition becomes
\[
 (\gamma-\beta)^2+16\delta\lambda=0.
\]
Real material coefficients with \(\delta\lambda>0\) therefore
exclude the elliptic sector. The rational-exponential sector is
tested by \eqref{eq:ksmatching}, with rate and coefficient scales
restored.

\subsection{Kawahara: an even coefficient restriction}\label{sec:kawahara}

For the Kawahara equation
\citep{Kawahara1972,DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010},
the integrated, shifted and monic travelling-wave operator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kawahara-P}
 P=D^4+bD^2+a_0.
\end{equation}
Meromorphic completeness for this equation, using its first
integral, is already established by
\citet{DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010}; see also
\citet{YeEtAl2022}.
Their first integral is the order-four instance of \eqref{eq:energy}.
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} places that argument in the uniform
operator setting, with the energy retained when fixing a Laurent
expansion.

The even operators form the reflection-symmetric family of
Section~\ref{sec:reflection} at \(p=4\). By
Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}, an even operator gives a profile even
about its pole: reflection preserves both the equation and its energy,
so Laurent uniqueness applies.
In the logistic coordinate this is \(v(Q)=v(1-Q)\).
Since \(v(0)=0\), both endpoints are zero; the normalized polynomial
satisfies \(\iota A=A\), that is, \(A=\rho^3+c\rho\).
Its quotient and remainder are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kawahara-exponential}
 \begin{split}
 P_A&=\rho^4+13c\rho^2+\frac{31c^2-70c+7}{3},\\
 v_A&=280(1+c)Q(1-Q)-1680Q^2(1-Q)^2,\\
 \operatorname{rem}_A S_A
   &=-\frac{\rho}{420}(c+1)(31c^2-31c+10).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
The three distinct roots are
\[
 c=-1,\qquad c=\frac12\pm\frac{3i}{2\sqrt{31}}.
\]
They give the three simple rational-exponential pairs in this
coefficient family, in agreement with \(F_4=\binom31=3\). These are the three simply periodic parameter
choices of \citet[Section~3]{DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010},
in the present normalization: the parameter in their Eq.~(31)
equals \(1/c\) here. At \(c=-1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kawahara-soliton}
 v=-105\operatorname{sech}^4(z/2),\qquad
 P=(D^2-4)(D^2-9).
\end{equation}
This is the familiar solitary-wave branch of the Kawahara equation;
the other two pairs are complex at unit real rate.
The count three is for the even family \eqref{eq:kawahara-P}.
The unrestricted order-four count in Theorem~\ref{thm:prime} is 35.

All elliptic members of \eqref{eq:kawahara-P} have the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kawahara-elliptic}
 v=-1680\wp^2-\frac{280b}{13}\wp
       +168g_2+\frac{31b^2}{507}-a_0.
\end{equation}
Substitution gives exactly two conditions:
\begin{align}
 F(b;g_2,g_3)&:=31b^3-42588b g_2-4745520g_3=0,
       \label{eq:kawahara-cubic}\\
 a_0^2&=23184g_2^2+\frac{7980}{169}b^2g_2
                       +\frac{101520}{13}b g_3.
       \label{eq:kawahara-background}
\end{align}
These are the Weierstrass profiles and parameter relations obtained
from Laurent series by
\citet[Section~3]{DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010},
expressed in the normalization \eqref{eq:main}.
In their notation, \(b=-\beta\), \(a_0^2=C_0^2+12C_1\);
the profile change is \(v=-6(w-w_\ast)\), where \(w_\ast\)
is a constant equilibrium and \(a_0=C_0-6w_\ast\).
The same two equations give the fixed-lattice count directly:
the first is cubic in \(b\), and the second is monic
quadratic in \(a_0\).
Their quotient algebra has dimension six for every fixed lattice,
which is the length \(2F_4\) of
Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric}.
At \((g_2,g_3)=(1,0)\), the cubic has three distinct roots and
the right side of \eqref{eq:kawahara-background} is nonzero at
all of them. Thus the six points are simple on a nonempty open
set of lattices.
This is the count within the even family; the full order-four
elliptic matching scheme has length 30.
Elliptic formulas with nonzero odd derivative coefficients in the
full fifth-order evolution equation are given by
\citet{KudryashovSinelshchikov2012}.

For a fixed-operator illustration, set \(b=0\).
Equations \eqref{eq:kawahara-cubic}--\eqref{eq:kawahara-background}
then give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p4invariants}
 P=D^4+a_0,\qquad
 g_3=0,\qquad g_2=\pm\frac{a_0}{12\sqrt{161}}.
\end{equation}
If \(a_0\ne0\), both lattices are nonsingular.
The corresponding profiles
\[
 v=-1680\wp^2+168g_2-a_0
\]
have marked coefficients
\(h=[z^8]v=-21g_2^3/130\), and hence different energies.
There is no nonconstant rational or rational-exponential solution
for this fixed operator: at a singular cubic
\(g_2=q^2/12\), \(g_3=-q^3/216\), the first equation forces
\(q=0\), and the second forces \(a_0=0\).
When \(a_0=0\), only the rational profile \(v=-1680z^{-4}\)
remains.
These statements exhaust the nonconstant globally meromorphic
solutions for \(P=D^4+a_0\), up to translation.

At \(a_0=12\sqrt{161}\), \(g_2=1\), \(g_3=0\), a translate
by the imaginary half-period is the real bounded wave
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p4real}
 v(z)=-1680
 \left(-\frac12+\frac12\operatorname{sn}^2(z\mid1/2)\right)^2
 +168-12\sqrt{161}.
\end{equation}
Its real period is \(2K(1/2)\).
Figure~\ref{fig:kawahara} compares it with
\eqref{eq:kawahara-soliton}.
The energy parameter is what permits distinct lattices for a fixed
even operator, while preserving uniqueness at fixed marked data.
