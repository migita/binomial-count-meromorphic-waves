\section{Examples}\label{sec:examples}

The examples have one purpose: to show the counts of
Table~\ref{tab:named} at work, and to connect them with the classical
lists. Orders two and three are
small enough for the full family to be treated by hand
(Sections~\ref{sec:kdvb} and~\ref{sec:ks}); the second of them also
shows what multiplicity means for elliptic pairs. At higher orders we
list the pairs of the two pure subfamilies
(Sections~\ref{sec:kawahara} and~\ref{sec:p5}).
The normalizations are those of Section~\ref{sec:setting}.

\subsection{Order two: KdV--Burgers}\label{sec:kdvb}

The integrated travelling-wave equation for
\[
 u_t+uu_x+\nu u_{xx}+\delta u_{xxx}=0,\qquad \delta\ne0,
\]
has order \(p=2\).
Its exact front solutions have been studied in several equivalent
forms \citep{JeffreyXu1989,VliegHalford1991,Kudryashov2009Errors}.
The long-wave reductions in
\citet{KudryashovSinelshchikovChernyavsky2008}
also contain this balance of dissipation and dispersion.

Here \(A(\rho)=\rho+a\) has a single coefficient, and the matching
system is one equation in one unknown. Direct polynomial division
gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kdvb-matching}
 \operatorname{rem}_A S_A=-\frac{a(a-1)(a+1)}6,
 \qquad
 P_A=\rho^2+5a\rho+a^2-6a-1,
 \qquad
 v_A=12\bigl((a+1)Q-Q^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
Thus there are exactly \(N_2=3\) simple pairs:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kdvb-pairs}
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 a&P_A(\rho)&v_A\\ \hline
 -1&(\rho-2)(\rho-3)&-12Q^2\\
 0&\rho^2-1&12Q(1-Q)=3\operatorname{sech}^2(z/2)\\
 1&(\rho-1)(\rho+6)&12Q(2-Q).
 \end{array}
\end{equation}
The pulse \(a=0\) is the KdV soliton. It is the single pair of the
purely dispersive subfamily, \(F_2=1\). The two fronts belong to mixed
equations, and the reflection \(\iota\) exchanges them: for a given
equation with \(\nu\ne0\) they are one and the same front, read in the
two directions (Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed}). At unit rate they are
two different pairs, and the count \(N_2=3\) includes both.
In the original variables, \eqref{eq:scaling} and
\eqref{eq:coefficients} read
\[
 u(x,t)=u_\ast+\delta\kappa^2
            v_A\bigl(\kappa(x-ct)\bigr),\qquad
 \nu=\delta\kappa(5a),\qquad
 u_\ast-c=\delta\kappa^2(a^2-6a-1).
\]
In particular, a nonzero \(\nu\) fixes the front rate to
\(\kappa=\nu/(5a\delta)\), \(a=\pm1\), in agreement with the
classical parameter restriction.

For a fixed lattice the elliptic candidate is \(V=-12X+b\).
Its residual is
\[
 -12a_1Y-12(a_0+b)X+6g_2+a_0b+\frac12b^2.
\]
Consequently the complete elliptic system is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kdvb-elliptic}
 a_1=0,\qquad
 v=-12\wp-a_0,\qquad a_0^2=12g_2.
\end{equation}
There is no one-pole elliptic member with nonzero Burgers coefficient:
both elliptic pairs belong to the KdV equation, in agreement with
\(M_2=2F_2=2\). They are the two backgrounds \eqref{eq:shift} of one
cnoidal wave. For \(g_2\ne0\) they are distinct and simple.
For \(g_2=0\), \(g_3\ne0\), they coincide, and the lattice carries one
pair of multiplicity two.

Since \(2p-1=3\) is prime, the counts at order two are unconditional.
The classification of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} applies to the KdV
subfamily \(a_1=0\). For the mixed operators, the Fisher example in
Section~\ref{sec:open} explains why the same conclusion cannot be
expected.

\subsection{Order three: Kuramoto--Sivashinsky}\label{sec:ks}

Let \(p=3\), \(s_3=60\), and \(A(\rho)=\rho^2+a\rho+b\).
The profile and monic operator are
\begin{align}
 \frac{v_A}{60}
   &=2Q^3-(a+3)Q^2+(a+b+1)Q, \label{eq:ksprofile}\\
 P_A(\rho)
   &=\rho^3+4a\rho^2+(a^2+19b)\rho
            -a^3+7ab-5a-30b. \label{eq:ksoperator}
\end{align}
The remainder of \(S_A\) is \((E_1\rho+E_0)/30\), where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ksmatching}
 E_0=ab(a^2-7b+5),\qquad
 E_1=a^4-8a^2b+11b^2+10b-1.
\end{equation}
The system \(E_0=E_1=0\) is solved by cases on the factors of
\(E_0\).
If \(b=0\), then \(a^4=1\).
If \(a=0\), then \((b+1)(11b-1)=0\).
In the remaining case \(a^2=7b-5\), and the second equation becomes
\(4(b-2)(b-3)=0\).
This exhausts the system and gives Table~\ref{tab:ks}.
The Jacobian determinant is nonzero at every point, so all
\(N_3=10\) pairs are simple, as Theorem~\ref{thm:prime} predicts for
\(2p-1=5\).

The ten labels are the ten two-element subsets of
\(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\). The four pulses are the pairs whose label contains
\(0\), and the mirror image \((a,b)\mapsto(-a,b)\) replaces a label
\(S\) by \(-S\), as Proposition~\ref{cor:endpoints} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric} assert.
There are ten complex pairs, eight with real coefficients.
All eight are smooth and bounded on the real axis:
two pulses and six fronts.
For example,
\[
 A=(\rho-1)(\rho-2),\qquad
 v_A=120Q^3,\qquad
 P_A=(\rho-3)(\rho-4)(\rho-5).
\]
These are the classical solutions in hyperbolic functions
\citep{KuramotoTsuzuki1976,Kudryashov1988,Kudryashov1990}.
The complete parameter list is tabulated by
\citet[Table~1]{KudryashovZargaryan1996} and
\citet[Table~1]{ConteMusette2009}.
Their tables contain six parameter cases because they identify the
two signs of the dispersion coefficient. These six cases are the orbits
of the reflection \(\iota\), which is \((a,b)\mapsto(-a,b)\) here:
four orbits of two pairs, and the two fixed pairs with \(a=0\).
Exhaustion of this classical list follows from
\citet{Eremenko2006}.

The two pairs with \(a=0\) are the \(F_3=2\) pairs of the purely
dissipative subfamily, the Kuramoto--Sivashinsky equation without
dispersion. Both are fronts, as Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric} requires;
the pulses \((\pm1,0)\) need dispersion.
By Corollary~\ref{cor:solvable}, an equation with
\(P=D^3+BD^2+\mu D+a_0\) has a wave of this kind exactly when
\(B^2=\sigma\mu\) for one of the four values \eqref{eq:ks-sigma}.
The pairs \((\pm1,0)\) and \((\pm i,0)\) belong to the same
equations, with \(\sigma=16\); their rates differ by a factor \(i\), so
for a real equation the second wave is periodic in \(x\), and its real
form has poles on the real axis.

\paragraph{Elliptic pairs, and the lattices on which they merge.}
An elliptic solution must have zero residue at its pole. For
\(P=D^3+BD^2+\mu D+a_0\) the Laurent residue is
\(60\mu/19-15B^2/76\), so \(B^2=16\mu\). With this restriction the
entire elliptic matching scheme is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ks-elliptic-scheme}
 \begin{split}
 P&=D^3+B D^2+\frac{B^2}{16}D+a_0,\\
 v&=-60\wp'-15B\wp-\frac{B^3}{64}-a_0,\\
 B^4&=3072g_2,\qquad
 a_0^2=\frac{13B^6}{2048}-2160g_3.
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
Read with the operator fixed, this is the classical solution of
\citet{Kudryashov1990}: \(w=v+a_0\) solves the usual integrated equation
\(w'''+Bw''+\mu w'+w^2/2+K=0\) with \(K=-a_0^2/2\). Necessity of the
restriction \(B^2=16\mu\) is proved by \citet{Hone2005}, and the
classification of all meromorphic KS solutions is completed by
\citet{Eremenko2006}. For the count we read \eqref{eq:ks-elliptic-scheme}
the other way: fix \(g_2,g_3\) and solve for \(B\) and \(a_0\).

For a typical lattice, the equation for \(B\) has four solutions, and
the equation for \(a_0\) then gives two choices for each of them: the
two backgrounds \(\pm a_0\) of \eqref{eq:shift}. These are the
\(M_3=8\) pairs. When the lattice changes, some of the pairs can meet.
The multiplicity of the resulting point records how many nearby pairs
have come together, so the count with multiplicity can remain eight
even when fewer than eight distinct pairs remain.

Pairs meet exactly where the Jacobian of the last two equations of
\eqref{eq:ks-elliptic-scheme} vanishes, and that Jacobian is a nonzero
constant times \(B^3a_0\). Let
\[
 j(\Lambda)=\frac{1728g_2^3}{g_2^3-27g_3^2}.
\]
If \(B=0\), then \(g_2=0\), hence \(j=0\).
If \(a_0=0\) and the lattice is nonsingular, elimination gives
\[
 675g_3^2=169g_2^3,\qquad j=-300.
\]
These conditions are sufficient as well. Thus:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ks-collisions}
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 j(\Lambda)&\text{distinct pairs}&\text{total with multiplicity}\\ \hline
 j\notin\{0,-300\}&8&8\times1\\
 -300&6&2\times2+4\times1\\
 0&2&2\times4.
 \end{array}
\end{equation}
The two exceptional cases show the two ways in which pairs can meet.

At \(j=-300\), the four values of \(B\) remain distinct. For two of
them, the two choices of \(a_0\) meet at zero: the two backgrounds of
one wave coincide. Each produces one pair of multiplicity two, while
the other four pairs remain distinct. There are six distinct pairs,
with total multiplicity \(2+2+1+1+1+1=8\). For instance,
\(g_2=1/12\), \(g_3=13/1080\) gives the double points
\((B,a_0)=(\pm4,0)\).

At \(j=0\), all four values of \(B\) meet at \(B=0\). The two roots of
\(a_0^2=-2160g_3\) remain distinct, since \(g_3\ne0\), so there are two
pairs, each of multiplicity four. Both have the operator \(D^3+a_0\), which is purely
dissipative, and the total is again \(4+4=8\).

The count stays eight for an exact reason. The coefficients eliminated
on the way to \eqref{eq:ks-elliptic-scheme} are determined linearly,
with nonzero rational pivots, so its last two equations carry the full
matching system, multiplicities included. The first has four roots
\(B\) and, for each of them, the second has two roots \(a_0\), counted
with multiplicity. This gives eight solutions on every lattice. This is the form in which
Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count} holds on an arbitrary lattice, and
\eqref{eq:ks-collisions} is the exceptional set of that theorem at
order three.

\subsection{Purely dispersive equations: Kawahara and seventh-order KdV}
\label{sec:kawahara}

\paragraph{Order four.}
For the Kawahara equation
\citep{Kawahara1972,DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010},
the integrated, shifted and monic travelling-wave operator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kawahara-P}
 P=D^4+bD^2+a_0.
\end{equation}
The classification for this equation, using its first
integral, is already established by
\citet{DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010}; see also
\citet{YeEtAl2022}.
Their first integral is the order-four instance of \eqref{eq:energy}.

By Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric}, the pairs with an even operator are
those with \(\iota A=A\), that is, \(A=\rho^3+c\rho\). All of them are
pulses: the profile is even about its pole, \(v(Q)=v(1-Q)\).
The quotient and remainder are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kawahara-exponential}
 \begin{split}
 P_A&=\rho^4+13c\rho^2+\frac{31c^2-70c+7}{3},\\
 v_A&=280(1+c)Q(1-Q)-1680Q^2(1-Q)^2,\\
 \operatorname{rem}_A S_A
   &=-\frac{\rho}{420}(c+1)(31c^2-31c+10).
 \end{split}
\end{equation}
The three distinct roots are
\[
 c=-1,\qquad c=\frac12\pm\frac{3i}{2\sqrt{31}}.
\]
They give \(F_4=\binom31=3\) simple pairs. These are the three simply
periodic parameter choices of
\citet[Section~3]{DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010}, in the present
normalization: the parameter in their Eq.~(31) equals \(1/c\) here.
At \(c=-1\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kawahara-soliton}
 v=-105\operatorname{sech}^4(z/2),\qquad
 P=(D^2-4)(D^2-9).
\end{equation}
This is the familiar solitary wave of the Kawahara equation;
the other two pairs are complex at unit real rate.
The unrestricted order-four count is \(N_4=35\). Of these pairs,
\(21\) have real coefficients, and all \(21\) are rational: five pulses
and sixteen fronts, which are eleven wave shapes up to reflection. They
are the one-pole waves of the mixed fifth-order equations, such as the
Benney--Lin equation, and the repository dictionary lists them.

All elliptic members of \eqref{eq:kawahara-P} have the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kawahara-elliptic}
 v=-1680\wp^2-\frac{280b}{13}\wp
       +168g_2+\frac{31b^2}{507}-a_0.
\end{equation}
Substitution gives exactly two conditions:
\begin{align}
 F(b;g_2,g_3)&:=31b^3-42588b g_2-4745520g_3=0,
       \label{eq:kawahara-cubic}\\
 a_0^2&=23184g_2^2+\frac{7980}{169}b^2g_2
                       +\frac{101520}{13}b g_3.
       \label{eq:kawahara-background}
\end{align}
These are the Weierstrass profiles and parameter relations obtained
from Laurent series by
\citet[Section~3]{DeminaKudryashovKawahara2010},
expressed in the normalization \eqref{eq:main}.
In their notation, \(b=-\beta\), \(a_0^2=C_0^2+12C_1\);
the profile change is \(v=-6(w-w_\ast)\), where \(w_\ast\)
is a constant equilibrium and \(a_0=C_0-6w_\ast\).
The first equation is cubic in \(b\), and the second is monic
quadratic in \(a_0\), so there are \(3\times2=6\) solutions, counted
with multiplicity, on every fixed lattice: three values of \(b\), each
with its two backgrounds. This is the number \(2F_4\) of
Corollary~\ref{cor:elliptic-symmetric}.
At \((g_2,g_3)=(1,0)\), the cubic has three distinct roots and
the right side of \eqref{eq:kawahara-background} is nonzero at
all of them, so the six pairs are simple on a nonempty open
set of lattices.
The unrestricted elliptic count at this order is \(M_4=30\); elliptic
formulas with nonzero odd derivative coefficients in the full
fifth-order evolution equation are given by
\citet{KudryashovSinelshchikov2012}.

\paragraph{Order six.}
The seventh-order KdV equation has the operator
\(P=D^6+a_4D^4+a_2D^2+a_0\); its meromorphic travelling waves are
studied by \citet{Dang2023Even}.
Here \(A=\rho^5+c_1\rho^3+c_2\rho\) and \(s_6=5544\). The remainder of
\(S_A\) has two coefficients, at \(\rho^3\) and at \(\rho\), which are
polynomials in \(c_1,c_2\) of weights eight and ten; the ancillary
script prints them. Eliminating \(c_2\), which is determined by \(c_1\)
at every solution, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p6-eliminant}
 (c_1+5)(c_1+10)(49c_1^2-5c_1+10)\,g_6(c_1)=0,
\end{equation}
where \(g_6\) is an irreducible sextic without real roots, also printed
by the script. The ten roots are distinct, in
agreement with \(F_6=\binom52=10\). Two pairs are real:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p6-real}
 \begin{array}{c|c|c}
 A&P_A&v_A\\ \hline
 \rho(\rho^2-1)(\rho^2-4)&(\rho^2-9)(\rho^2-16)(\rho^2-25)
   &10395\operatorname{sech}^6(z/2)\\
 \rho(\rho^2-1)(\rho^2-9)&(\rho^2-4)(\rho^2-25)(\rho^2-71)
   &10395\operatorname{sech}^4(z/2)\bigl(1+\operatorname{sech}^2(z/2)\bigr).
 \end{array}
\end{equation}
All ten pairs are pulses. An exact computation on a sample lattice
gives \(2F_6=20\) for the elliptic pairs of this family.

\subsection{Purely dissipative equations of order five}\label{sec:p5}

At \(p=5\) the purely dissipative operators are
\(P=D^5+a_3D^3+a_1D+a_0\), and \(A=\rho^4+c_1\rho^2+c_2\).
All \(126\) pairs at \(p=5\) are simple
(Section~\ref{sec:further}), so there are exactly \(F_5=6\) distinct
pairs here.
They are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:p5-symmetric}
 A=(\rho^2-1)(\rho^2-4),\qquad
 A=(\rho^2-1)(\rho^2-9),\qquad
 A=(\rho^2-1)\Bigl(\rho^2+\frac15\Bigr),
\end{equation}
and the three pairs with
\[
 1105c_1^3-2969c_1^2+5570c_1-4000=0,\qquad
 c_2=\frac{5c_1^3+21c_1-10}{79c_1+210}.
\]
The cubic has one real root. All six pairs are fronts, as
Theorem~\ref{thm:symmetric} requires, and the four with real
coefficients are, after the scales are restored, the solutions~(1.4)
of \citet{KudryashovMigita2007}. For instance,
\(A=(\rho^2-1)(\rho^2-4)\) gives
\[
 v_A=5040\,Q^3\bigl(10-15Q+6Q^2\bigr),\qquad
 P_A=\rho^5-55\rho^3+1254\rho-2520 ,
\]
a front from \(0\) to \(5040=-2P_A(0)\).
