\section{What the count means for a single equation}\label{sec:fixed}

We read the count for one equation \eqref{eq:pde} with fixed complex
coefficients. It is invariant under translations and Galilean shifts.

\begin{proposition}[The waves of a fixed equation]\label{prop:fixed}
Let \(\kappa\ne0\). Up to a translation and a Galilean shift, the
travelling waves of \eqref{eq:pde} that are rational in
\(e^{\kappa(x-ct)}\), with one pole modulo \((2\pi i/\kappa)\ZZ\), are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-wave}
 u=b_p\kappa^p\,v_A\bigl(\kappa(x-ct)\bigr),\qquad c=-b_p\kappa^pP_A(0),
\end{equation}
where \(A\) ranges over the points of \(\Xp\) that satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-conditions}
 \frac{b_j}{b_p}=\kappa^{p-j}\,[\rho^j]P_A,\qquad 1\le j\le p-1 .
\end{equation}
The data \((A,\kappa)\) and \((\iota A,-\kappa)\) describe the same
wave, read in the two directions (Section~\ref{sec:reflection}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Such a wave has a finite limit as \(e^{\kappa(x-ct)}\to0\)
(Section~\ref{sec:systems}), and a Galilean shift makes it zero. Then
\(w_\ast=0\) in \eqref{eq:scaling}, and \eqref{eq:coefficients} becomes
\eqref{eq:fixed-conditions} and the formula for \(c\). After a
translation \(v\) has the normalization of Section~\ref{sec:setting}, so
\(v=v_A\) and \(P=P_A\) by Proposition~\ref{prop:divisibility}. The last
assertion follows from \eqref{eq:reflection-pair}: the two waves differ
by the constant \(b_p\kappa^ps_pA(0)\), which is a Galilean shift.
\end{proof}

Given a pair, \eqref{eq:fixed-conditions} are \(p-1\) conditions on the
\(p-1\) ratios \(b_j/b_p\) and the one unknown \(\kappa\): a pair
imposes \(p-2\) conditions on the equation and fixes the rate. A pair
with real coefficients gives a real wave of a real equation, smooth on
the real axis, exactly when \(\kappa\) is real. Let
\(\mathbf b=(b_{p-1}/b_p,\ldots,b_1/b_p)\) and
\(\mathbf a(A)=([\rho^{p-1}]P_A,\ldots,[\rho^{1}]P_A)\) be the
coefficient vectors of the equation and of \(P_A\). By
\eqref{eq:fixed-conditions}, \(\mathbf b\) is \(\mathbf a(A)\) with its
\(k\)th entry multiplied by \(\kappa^k\). As \(\kappa\) varies these
vectors trace a curve \(\CC^\ast\mathbf a(A)\), and rescaling \(x\)
moves an equation along it. Several pairs may give the same curve.

\begin{corollary}[Solvable equations]\label{cor:solvable}
Assume that the matching equations have finitely many solutions. This
holds when \(2p-1\) is prime, and whenever rigidity holds
(Proposition~\ref{prop:bezout}), hence for every \(p\le573\)
(Section~\ref{sec:further}).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(i)}] Equation \eqref{eq:pde} has a travelling wave that
is rational in an exponential, with one pole per period, if and only if
\(\mathbf b\) lies on one of the curves \(\CC^\ast\mathbf a(A)\),
\(A\in\Xp\). There are at most \((N_p+F_p)/2\) such curves.
\item[\textup{(ii)}] If \(\mathbf b\ne0\), there are finitely many such
waves up to translations and Galilean shifts.
\item[\textup{(iii)}] If \(p\ge3\), the curves form a set of dimension
at most one in \(\CC^{p-1}\), and a generic equation has no such wave.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Part~(i) restates Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed}. For the bound,
\(\mathbf a(\iota A)=(-1)\cdot\mathbf a(A)\) by
\eqref{eq:reflection-pair}, so \(A\) and \(\iota A\) give the same
curve. The number of classes \(\{A,\iota A\}\) is half the number of
points plus half the number of fixed points. The solutions are finite in
number, hence isolated, so these two numbers are at most \(N_p\) and
\(F_p\) (Proposition~\ref{prop:bezout}, Remark~\ref{rem:Fp-bound}).
For~(ii), if \(\mathbf b\ne0\) and \(\mathbf b\in\CC^\ast\mathbf a(A)\),
some \([\rho^j]P_A\) is nonzero, and \eqref{eq:fixed-conditions} leaves
at most \(p-j\) values of \(\kappa\). Part~(iii) is a dimension count.
\end{proof}

When \(\mathbf b=0\) the equation is scale-invariant, and each of its
waves occurs at every rate. At order two there are two curves, and both
carry waves: \(\mathbf b=0\) is KdV, with its solitons, and each
equation with \(\mathbf b\ne0\) has one front, its rate fixed by the
Burgers coefficient (Section~\ref{sec:kdvb}).

At \(p=3\), write \(P=D^3+BD^2+\mu D+a_0\), so that
\(\mathbf b=(B,\mu)\). Rescaling multiplies \(B\) by \(\kappa\) and
\(\mu\) by \(\kappa^2\), so the curves are \(B^2=\sigma^2\mu\) with the
origin removed, where \(\sigma\) is the dispersion coefficient of the
Introduction. By the caption of Table~\ref{tab:ks}, the ten pairs give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ks-sigma}
 \sigma^2=\frac{16a^2}{a^2+19b}\in\{0,\ 16,\ 144/47,\ 256/73\},\qquad
 |\sigma|\in\{0,\ 4,\ 12/\sqrt{47},\ 16/\sqrt{73}\},
\end{equation}
the four values of \citet[Eq.~(39)]{Kudryashov1989}. The bound
\((N_3+F_3)/2=6\) is not attained: the two pairs with \(a=0\) share
\(\sigma=0\), and \((\pm1,0)\) and \((\pm i,0)\) share \(\sigma^2=16\).
For coefficients off these curves, \citet{KudryashovKochanov2012}
construct approximate solutions close to the exact ones.

\begin{corollary}[Generic equations with \(p\) odd]\label{cor:generic}
Let \(p\ge5\) be odd and assume rigidity \((\mathrm R_{p-1})\), which
holds when \(2p-1\) is prime and for every \(p\le573\). The coefficient
vectors \(\mathbf b\in\CC^{p-1}\) for which \eqref{eq:pde} has a
nonconstant meromorphic travelling wave lie in an algebraic subset of
dimension at most two. In particular a generic equation has none.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}, such a wave is rational, rational in an
exponential, or elliptic, with one pole per period. Take \(\kappa=1\) in
\eqref{eq:scaling}, so that \([\rho^j]P=b_j/b_p\) for \(1\le j\le p-1\).
The rational-exponential waves give a set of dimension at most one
(Corollary~\ref{cor:solvable}). A rational wave is, after a translation,
of the form \eqref{eq:rational-system}; by Lemma~\ref{lem:laplace} it
comes from a monic \(A\) of degree \(p-1\) that divides \(H_A\), and
rigidity gives \(A=\rho^{p-1}\), hence \(P=D^p\) and \(\mathbf b=0\).
For the elliptic waves, Steps~1--3 of the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:elliptic-count} use only rigidity, so by
Lemma~\ref{lem:conservation}(i) every system \(\mathcal E_p(g_2,g_3)\)
has finitely many solutions. The solutions for all \((g_2,g_3)\)
together thus form an algebraic set of dimension at most two, and so
does the closure of its image under \(P\mapsto\mathbf b\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Order three]\label{rem:ks-curves}
At \(p=3\) the argument gives the exact answer: these
\(\mathbf b=(B,\mu)\) form the union of the four curves
\(B^2=\sigma^2\mu\) of \eqref{eq:ks-sigma}. The elliptic waves require
\(B^2=16\mu\) (Section~\ref{sec:ks}), which is one of the four, and the
point \(\mathbf b=0\) of the rational wave lies on all of them.
\end{remark}

The same dimension count applies to the one-pole waves of every order
\(p\ge3\) for which rigidity holds: a generic equation has no travelling
wave that is rational, rational in an exponential, or elliptic with one
pole per period. For even \(p\), however, Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}
excludes other meromorphic waves only for purely dispersive equations.

Can one equation have two different waves of this kind at the same rate
and speed? It cannot, even for the mixed equations of odd order, to
which Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} does not apply.

\begin{proposition}[One profile per operator]\label{prop:injective}
Let \(A_1,A_2\) be monic polynomials of degree \(p-1\) satisfying
\(A_1\mid S_{A_1}\) and \(A_2\mid S_{A_2}\). If \(P_{A_1}=P_{A_2}\),
then \(A_1=A_2\). Consequently a monic operator of degree \(p\) admits
at most one normalized rational-exponential one-pole profile, and any
count of distinct normalized pairs is also a count of distinct operators.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Suppose \(P_{A_1}=P_{A_2}=P\) and \(h=A_1-A_2\ne0\). The degree \(n\) of
\(h\) is less than \(p-1\); write \(f\) for its leading coefficient. By
\eqref{eq:operator-A} and the bilinearity and symmetry of the
convolution, \(S_{A_1}-S_{A_2}=h\star(A_1+A_2)=(2/s_p)Ph\). The
power-sum calculation in \eqref{eq:Sleading} gives the leading
coefficient \(2f\int_0^1t^n(1-t)^{p-1}\dd t\) on the left. Since \(P\)
is monic, the right side has the leading coefficient \((2/s_p)f\), and
\(2/s_p\) is the beta integral \(\int_0^1t^{p-1}(1-t)^{p-1}\dd t\) of
\eqref{eq:Sleading}. This is impossible: \(n<p-1\) implies
\(t^n>t^{p-1}\) for \(0<t<1\), so the first integral exceeds the second,
and the left coefficient is more than twice the right one.
\end{proof}

Every \(P_A\) has a root among \(1,\ldots,p\). Let \(m\) be the least
positive integer with \(A(m)\ne0\); then \(m\le p\), because \(A\) has
degree \(p-1\). As \(e^z\to0\) the profile begins with
\(s_p(-1)^{m-1}A(m)e^{mz}\) and its square with \(e^{2mz}\), so the
coefficient of \(e^{mz}\) in \eqref{eq:main} gives \(P_A(m)=0\).
Physically, the wave leaves its background like \(e^{mz}\), so \(m\) is
a spatial eigenvalue of the linearization there, a root of \(P\).

\paragraph{Other equations with the same profile equation.}
The reduction of Section~\ref{sec:setting} uses only the travelling-wave
variable, so the counts apply to any equation
\(\mathcal L u+\mathcal M(u^2)=0\) with constant-coefficient operators,
mixed derivatives and several space variables included, whose plane-wave
reduction integrates to \eqref{eq:main}; the coefficients of \(P\) then
depend on the speed and the direction of the wave. An example in three
space variables is the equation of
\citet{KudryashovSinelshchikovThreeDim2012} for a liquid with gas
bubbles, whose reduction is that of KdV--Burgers. The repository lists
such equations by profile operator (\texttt{tools/wave\_dictionary/}).

\paragraph{Ansatz methods.}
In standard form (one phase \(kx-\omega t\), constant coefficients) the
tanh, sech, exp-function and \((G'/G)\)-expansion methods and the
simplest-equation method with a Riccati equation
\citep{Kudryashov2005Simplest} return functions rational in one
exponential, and the Jacobi and Weierstrass expansions
\citep{Kudryashov2009Errors} elliptic functions of one argument, with
rational functions as limits. An output with one pole per period (for
the equations of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}, every nonconstant output)
is, up to the symmetries of Proposition~\ref{prop:fixed}, a wave
\eqref{eq:fixed-wave}, an elliptic wave of
Section~\ref{sec:elliptic-counting} or the rational wave. So the counts
are a checklist: at a covered order, once the rate or the period lattice
is fixed, a tanh-type or elliptic ansatz with one pole per period can
return at most \(N_p\), respectively \(M_p\), normalized solutions,
whatever the method. The critical comparisons
\citep{KudryashovLoguinova2009,Kudryashov2009Errors,KudryashovSoukharev2009}
agree: the ``new'' KdV--Burgers and Kuramoto--Sivashinsky waves coincide
with the known ones \citep{Kudryashov2009KdVB,KudryashovSoukharev2009},
and an ansatz of pole order lower than \(p\) returns no wave
\citep{Kudryashov2010Kawahara}. The conformable derivative
\(T_\alpha f(t)=t^{1-\alpha}f'(t)\), \(t>0\), \(0<\alpha\le1\)
\citep{KhalilEtAl2014}, is \(\mathrm d/\mathrm d\tau\) for
\(\tau=t^\alpha/\alpha\), and similarly in space, so a transformed
equation with profile equation \eqref{eq:main} obeys the same counts;
this does not apply to the Caputo or Riemann--Liouville derivatives.
